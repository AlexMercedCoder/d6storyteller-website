## 1. Setting Overview & Tone

Glass cities were meant to end corruption. Instead, they made the visible life of poor citizens cheap entertainment while the powerful learned to hide inside procedure. A public can watch a worker eat dinner, but cannot see the closed algorithms that assign their rent. The characters belong to the quiet economy that restores a little darkness: blind spots, sealed messages, and trust that cannot be subpoenaed.

**Tone:** Tense, political, and uncomfortably relatable. The Republic is not a cartoon dystopia. Most of its citizens genuinely believe transparency makes society better. They are partially right. The setting asks where the line falls between accountability and control, and what happens when the people drawing that line are the same people who benefit from its position.

**Sensory Pillars:**
1. **Sight:** Buildings with transparent walls, interiors visible from the street like museum dioramas. Status indicators on every door: green means the occupant has opted in to public viewing (mandatory for government employees), blue means standard civic transparency, amber means the occupant has logged a temporary exception (allowed during medical care and grief). The absence of curtains, which are not illegal but are socially unacceptable.
2. **Sound:** The constant low tone of recording indicators built into public infrastructure. The click of an inspection scanner accepting a citizen's badge. The specific silence in a soundproofed room, which is contraband and instantly recognizable to anyone who has heard it.
3. **Touch & Smell:** The smooth, cold surface of glass walls that are always slightly damp from condensation. The chemical smell of optical coating that makes surfaces transparent in one direction, which the Opaque Line uses to create one-way privacy. The weight of an opaque case in your hands, knowing that carrying it marks you as someone with something to hide.

## 2. The City

**The Capitol Lens** is the government center: a cluster of fully transparent buildings where every meeting, negotiation, and vote is visible to any citizen with a viewing pass. The architecture is impressive and deliberately exposed. What is not visible: the encrypted internal communication channels that senior officials use, which are technically public record but require a court order to access.

**The Residential Stacks** are apartment towers with transparent exterior walls and optional interior partitions. Most residents leave partitions retracted because the social cost of privacy is higher than the cost of exposure. Families eat, argue, sleep, and live in view of their neighbors. Children grow up assuming this is normal. The adults remember when it was not.

**The Maintenance Tunnels** run beneath the city. They were built for infrastructure access and are the only spaces in the Republic without recording equipment. The Opaque Line operates here: moving privacy tools, maintaining hidden rooms, and running a courier network that exists in the gaps between sensors. The tunnels smell like electrical insulation and damp concrete.

**The Records Bazaar** sits beneath the central archive. Any citizen's public history is available for purchase: financial records, movement data, communication logs, social connections. It is legal to buy. It is illegal to alter. The Bazaar attracts journalists, investigators, jealous partners, and corporate researchers. Its brokers know more about the city's population than the Office of Civic Light, and they charge accordingly.

## 3. Factions

**The Office of Civic Light** runs the inspection network. Its junior inspectors spend their days watching tenants and shopkeepers while senior officials hide policy inside legal procedure. Director Kessler believes in the Republic's mission and is frustrated by the inspectors who use their access for personal leverage. She cannot fire them without exposing the complaints, which would reveal the complainants, which would endanger the people who trusted the system enough to report abuse.

**The Opaque Line** moves privacy tools through maintenance tunnels: blank film, sound-dampening paint, old-fashioned locks, and rooms where a person may speak without leaving a record. Its members argue over whether privacy should be sold or treated as mutual aid. The Opaque Line's founder, a former architect named Dex, designed many of the buildings she now helps people hide inside.

**The Open Hand Party** advocates honest transparency and is beginning to realize how selectively it is enforced. It can open hearings and attract crowds, but it is full of people who have never needed a place to hide. Its leader, Councilor Voss, has introduced legislation requiring algorithmic transparency for government systems. The legislation has been blocked three times by committees whose deliberations are, technically, public.

**The Records Bazaar** operates as a quasi-legal marketplace where information is currency. Its most powerful broker, known only as the Ledger, can assemble a complete profile of any citizen in the Republic within 24 hours. The Ledger sells to everyone and is loyal to no one, which makes the Bazaar the most dangerous neutral ground in the city.

## 4. Unique Mechanic: Exposure

Start every operation with Exposure 0. Mark +1 for public actions, betrayed confidence, traceable evidence, and anything that creates a record linking the character to unauthorized activity.

**Thresholds:**
- **At 2:** People who should not know begin asking specific questions. Social discomfort.
- **At 4:** Media attention or a hostile audit limits safe choices. The character's public history is being actively reviewed.
- **At 6:** The Office may inspect the character's home, contacts, and recent actions. This inspection is legal, thorough, and public.

**Reducing Exposure:** Create a lawful explanation for the flagged activity (-1). Take action through a trusted intermediary who absorbs the visibility (-1, but the intermediary gains +1). Make a public accusation the state cannot ignore, redirecting scrutiny (-2, but creates an enemy). To clear Exposure entirely, the characters must change who has the power to look.

**Hiding only delays the problem.** Unexplained gaps in a citizen's public record generate automatic flags. Silence is suspicious. The system is designed to notice absence.

## 5. Character Archetypes

### The Former Inspector

You know how to conduct a lawful search and exactly how much damage one can do. You left the Office after an inspection you conducted destroyed a family. The family was hiding a medical condition that would have resulted in mandatory quarantine. You found it. You reported it. The family was separated. The condition was treatable. You cannot stop thinking about what the right choice should have been.

- **Drive:** *"Turn the tools I used against ordinary people toward someone who chose to build them."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 2 | Mind 3 | Presence 2

### The Privacy Courier

You move secrets for people who do not have the luxury of being publicly misunderstood. You carry opaque cases through the maintenance tunnels, deliver sealed messages, and maintain safe rooms. You do not read what you carry. You do not ask why. Your clients include abuse survivors, political dissidents, whistleblowers, and occasionally people whose reasons for privacy are not sympathetic. You carry their secrets anyway.

- **Drive:** *"Keep one family out of the Records Bazaar long enough for them to leave the city."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 2 | Finesse 3 | Mind 1 | Presence 2

### The Open Hand Journalist

You write for the city's most-read transparency advocacy publication. You believe in the Republic's founding principle. You also believe the principle is being applied to the wrong people. Your current investigation involves an algorithm that assigns housing costs based on a citizen's viewing compliance score. The algorithm is public record. Its source code is classified. The contradiction is the story, and you are struggling to get anyone to care about a paradox when they are busy watching their neighbors.

- **Drive:** *"Make the system transparent to itself. If the Republic means what it says, it should be able to survive being seen."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 2 | Mind 2 | Presence 3

## 6. Starter Scenario & Why It Matters

**The Opaque Room:** A new black-market chamber has appeared in the heart of the capitol. Nobody can see inside it, including its builder. The room is perfectly opaque: no signal enters, no signal leaves, and the walls resist every scanning technology the Office has deployed. A senior minister entered the room four hours ago and has not emerged. The Office orders the entire block dismantled, which would expose every person who has used the Opaque Line's tunnel network in the past year. The Opaque Line wants the room preserved as proof that privacy can exist. The Open Hand Party wants the minister found but opposes the block demolition on civil liberty grounds. The Records Bazaar is already selling access to the viewing feeds from adjacent buildings. The characters must find the minister, determine what the room is and who built it, and decide whether a space that cannot be observed deserves to exist in a Republic that was founded on the principle that everything should be.

**Why It Matters:** The Glass Republic interrogates the assumption that visibility equals safety. The Republic is not wrong that transparency reduces certain kinds of corruption. It is wrong about who pays the cost. Privacy in the Republic is not a luxury. It is a human need that has been criminalized because it is inconvenient for the people who benefit from watching. The characters will not dismantle the Republic. They will decide, person by person and secret by secret, what deserves to be hidden and what deserves to be seen.
