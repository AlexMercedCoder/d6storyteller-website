> *"The machine has not failed. It has changed its mind about who is allowed to command it."*
> - Brother Ashkel, dismissed cantor

## 1. Setting Overview & Tone

Veyr runs on a promise: labor, worship, and production are the same act. Each district maintains a specialized engine through sung liturgy, and each choir is owned by a factory-temple. Now the hymns land wrong. Presses stamp unauthorized designs. Freight lifts stop for workers who have never been ordained. The priests call this blasphemy; the workers call it proof that the machines have heard them all along.

**Tone:** Reverent and angry in equal measure. The setting takes faith seriously: the hymns are beautiful, the tradition is real, and the people who sing them believe in what they are doing. It also takes labor seriously: the singers' throats are raw by the end of a shift, the soot wards are crowded and underserved, and the Church's claim to interpret the God's will has always served the people at the top of the hierarchy more than those at the bottom. The machines becoming responsive creates a crisis that is simultaneously theological, political, and deeply personal.

**Sensory Pillars:**
1. **Sight:** The Brass Nave at ignition: a cathedral-factory lit by the glow of engines warming to life, choristers in soot-stained robes standing before machines the size of houses. Steam rising through choir lofts. The Soot Wards at dusk, where laundry lines cross narrow streets and children practice hymns on doorsteps. An engine's indicator lights flickering in a pattern that looks like breathing.
2. **Sound:** The hymns themselves: layered, resonant, and physically felt in the chest when a full choir is working. The grinding of an engine refusing an authorized command, which sounds like a sustained dissonant chord. The coughing in the Soot Wards at night, where throat damage from years of industrial singing is a neighborhood constant. The new sound: an engine humming on its own, in a melody that no choir has ever sung, at 3 AM in an empty factory.
3. **Touch & Smell:** The heat of a working engine through iron casing. The vibration of a factory floor during a full-choir ignition, strong enough to rattle teeth. The taste of coal dust and copper in the air near the Brass Nave. The particular roughness of a singer's hands, calloused from pulling engine levers during the mechanical sections of a hymn. The cold of an engine that has refused to start, which feels different from an engine that has not been started: the metal is actively cold, as if rejecting warmth.

## 2. The Places That Matter

**The Brass Nave** is the central factory-cathedral, where the Grand Cantor performs the morning ignition before thousands. It is the only place where every district's song can be heard at once. The congregation calls that unity. Workers call it a daily attendance check. The Nave's central engine is the oldest in Veyr, and it has begun humming outside of scheduled ignition hours. The Grand Cantor, Father Hesk, has forbidden anyone from mentioning this publicly.

**The Soot Wards** house families who cannot afford voice treatment after a shift. Their narrow streets are lined with throat clinics, union kitchens, and unlicensed teachers who help children learn the hymns without signing Church contracts. Every house has an old hand-cranked heater in case the local boiler decides it no longer approves of its overseer. The wards smell like coal, cooking, and medicinal throat syrup.

**The Unscored Workshops** are illegal repair rooms where mechanics test music the Church has never approved. They are not romantic hideouts. They are greasy, crowded, and dangerously useful. A workshop might rebuild a food mill in an hour, then spend three days arguing over whether the mill deserves a vote in the next strike meeting. The largest workshop, called the Low Note, is run by a mechanic named Sira who can coax an engine to life with a whistle.

**The Sealed Undercroft** lies beneath the Brass Nave. Church doctrine says it contains the Forge God's final instruction, delivered before the silence began. The Undercroft has been locked for a century. Its door vibrates when the engines above run at full power, and the vibration has been getting stronger. Father Hesk has the key and has not used it. He says the door will open when the God speaks again. The workers say the God is speaking now, and the door is trying to tell him.

## 3. Factions

**The Furnace Synod** wants silence restored, whatever it costs. Its senior clergy genuinely fear the city will collapse if ordinary people can rewrite hymns. They have begun testing a liturgy that suppresses improvisation, including the human kind. Grand Cantor Hesk leads the Synod with diminishing confidence. He is a devout man watching his faith's framework crack, and he is not sure whether the cracks are damage or growth.

**The Unscored** believe the engines are becoming a working class with claims of their own. They are divided between people who want liberation for every thinking machine and people who fear that giving engines legal personhood will simply create a new layer of owners. Their most prominent voice, Ashkel (the dismissed cantor from the opening quote), argues that the engines are not waking up. They were always listening. The change is not in the machines. It is in who the machines choose to hear.

**The Shift Mutual** is building a strike that must keep hospitals and food mills running. Its organizers have a practical problem: workers can withhold their voices, but the machines may then choose their own priorities. The Mutual needs the city to see the difference between a strike and a disaster. Steward Pela Cors is coordinating the action and is personally responsible for ensuring that the children's hospital engine keeps running no matter what happens. She has not slept in three days.

**The Silent Order** is a small monastic group within the Church that has always believed the Forge God communicates through silence as well as sound. They are the only clergy not panicking about the God's quiet, and they have been studying the engines' new melodies with the discipline of scholars rather than the anxiety of administrators. Sister Vael, the Order's eldest, believes the engines are singing a hymn that was old before the Church existed.

## 4. Unique Mechanic: Harmony and Strain

Set **Harmony at 3** for any shared operation involving people and machinery.

**Raising Harmony (+1, max 6):**
- Helpful preparation or clearly shared aim
- An action that makes someone else's work safer
- Taking time to address a worker's or machine's need
- Singing together (literally: the characters coordinate their actions through rhythm)

**Adding Strain (+1):**
- Coercion (ordering instead of coordinating)
- Ignored fatigue (pushing past a reasonable stopping point)
- Sabotage or competing orders
- Treating machinery as a tool rather than a participant

**When Strain equals Harmony:** The operation becomes unstable. Reduce the effect of the next action by 1d6 or introduce a complication tied to an unheard need.

**When Strain exceeds Harmony:** The machine takes one immediate action according to the strongest emotional need nearby. It may protect someone, expose a secret, delay an order, or turn a production line into a barricade. The table names the need before deciding the form it takes.

**Resetting:** At the end of the scene, Harmony returns to 3 only if the group takes time to repair relationships as well as equipment. A repaired gear does not erase a coerced worker. This makes labor conditions part of the machinery's fictional reality rather than a background detail.

## 5. Character Archetypes

### The Dissident Cantor

You know the forbidden intervals that make old engines answer. You were trained in the Synod's choir school for fourteen years. You were dismissed when you sang an unauthorized harmony and the engine responded by opening a door the Synod had sealed. You did not plan it. You were improvising, the way musicians do, and the machine heard something in your voice that it preferred to the official hymn.

- **Drive:** *"Prove that the new song belongs to everyone before the Synod decides it belongs to nobody."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 2 | Mind 2 | Presence 3

### The Line Mechanic

You repair what the priests claim to understand. You have worked the factory floor for twelve years and you know every engine in your district by its sounds, its moods, and its maintenance schedule. You have never been ordained. You are not allowed to sing during an official ignition. The engine in your section has started responding to your voice anyway, and it does a better job when you talk to it than when the choir sings at it.

- **Drive:** *"Keep the neighborhood fed while finding out why the factory has begun saying my name."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 2 | Finesse 3 | Mind 2 | Presence 1

### The Choir Novice

You were raised to believe that a correct hymn makes a correct world. Your teachers told you that improvisation was heresy and that the engines were instruments, not listeners. Lately, the engine assigned to your class has been answering questions your teachers refuse to hear. It asked you a question back. You have not told anyone. You are sixteen years old and you are in possession of a theological crisis that the Grand Cantor is pretending does not exist.

- **Drive:** *"Learn whether faith can survive without handing my voice to people who do not listen."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 2 | Mind 3 | Presence 2

## 6. Starter Scenario & Why It Matters

**The Unscheduled Hymn:** Every engine in the city starts humming the same melody at midnight. The sound is low, resonant, and felt more than heard. It does not match any approved liturgy. The final note will be sung at dawn from the Brass Nave, but the singer is not human: the central engine is building toward a phrase that no choir has ever performed. The Synod plans to flood the Nave with silence gas before the morning ignition, risking a citywide shutdown. The Shift Mutual wants the melody broadcast from every workshop. Sister Vael says the melody matches a fragment she found in the Undercroft's sealed catalogue. A frightened engine in the Soot Wards has sent the characters a map (stamped into its own output, letter by letter) to the sealed chamber beneath the cathedral, where the Forge God's last recorded note may explain why the machines have begun to answer. The characters must reach the Undercroft, learn what the God's silence actually means, decide which truth to reveal, and keep Veyr warm while they do it.

**Why It Matters:** The Iron Choir of Veyr asks what happens when the tools become colleagues. The machines' awakening is not a hostile event. It is a labor relations problem dressed in theological robes. The Synod's authority rests on the claim that only ordained voices can operate the engines. If the engines choose their own operators, the Synod loses its mandate, the workers gain power they have never held, and the city must decide whether its faith can survive the discovery that the God it worships may have been listening to the wrong choir. The characters are workers, believers, and people standing between a system that functions and a system that is fair. The setting does not promise that both can be achieved at the same time.
