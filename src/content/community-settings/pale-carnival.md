## 1. Setting Overview & Tone

The Pale Carnival is beautiful in the way that a skeleton is beautiful: structurally precise, stripped of softness, and illuminated in a way that makes everything look clean and final. Its tents are white canvas with silver trim. Its performers wear pale costumes and move with choreographed grace. Its attractions are real: the strongman is genuinely strong, the fortune teller is genuinely accurate, and the games are genuinely fair. The Carnival does not cheat. It offers a straightforward transaction, and the customers agree to it because they do not understand what they are trading.

The characters are people who have encountered the Carnival: customers who made wishes and are living with the consequences, investigators trying to understand the Carnival's nature, or residents of the current host town watching their neighbors become subtly, specifically different.

**Tone:** Elegant, melancholic, and laced with the particular sadness of choices that seemed right at the time. The Carnival is not malicious. It is transactional. The performers are not cruel. They are professional. The tragedy is structural: the wishes work, the regrets are genuine losses, and the customers do not realize what they have given up until the absence has already changed them.

**Sensory Pillars:**
1. **Sight:** The Carnival at night: silver lights strung between white poles, casting a glow that makes everything look like moonlight. Performers in pale costumes, their movements fluid and slightly too synchronized, like dancers performing a routine they have rehearsed for longer than a human career. The Wish Tent: a white pavilion at the center of the fairground with a single chair, a single attendant, and a mirror that shows the customer's face, then shows it differently.
2. **Sound:** Carnival music that is beautiful and slightly wrong: a calliope that plays melodies one note off from recognizable songs. The laughter of customers, which is genuine but pitched higher than usual, as if they are performing enjoyment rather than experiencing it. The silence inside the Wish Tent, which is absolute and feels permanent.
3. **Touch & Smell:** The texture of the Carnival's prizes: stuffed animals, ribbons, and trinkets that feel lighter than they should, as if they are made from material that is not entirely present. The smell of carnival food (popcorn, spun sugar, roasted nuts) layered over something colder, like the air inside a cave. The warmth of the Wish Tent chair, which is body-temperature regardless of who sat in it last, as if it remembers everyone.

## 2. The Fairground

**The Wish Tent** is the Carnival's centerpiece. Inside: a white chair, an attendant in silver, and a full-length mirror. The customer sits, states their wish, and the attendant quotes the price: a specific future regret, described in enough detail that the customer can understand what they are trading. The attendant does not pressure. The attendant waits. The mirror shows the customer their face before and after the trade, which is the same face, but the "after" version is missing something the customer cannot identify until the wish is granted and the regret is gone.

**The Promenade** is the main walkway through the fairground: game booths, food stalls, and performance stages arranged in a circuit. The attractions are legitimate and entertaining. The Promenade serves a secondary purpose: it gives customers time to observe the Carnival's nature, ask questions, and decide whether to visit the Wish Tent. The Carnival does not rush. Time spent on the Promenade is time spent choosing.

**The Performers' Quarter** is the backstage area where the Carnival's staff rests between acts. It is not accessible to the public. Investigators who find their way in will discover that the performers live simply, quietly, and without personal possessions. They eat, sleep, and maintain their costumes. They do not appear to age. They are polite to intruders but will not answer questions about the Carnival's origins or purpose.

**The Collection Tent** is where the regrets are stored. It is located at the back of the fairground, unmarked, and locked. Inside: glass jars arranged on shelves, each one containing a small, luminous object that is warm to the touch and sounds, when held to the ear, like a specific human voice expressing a specific sorrow. The jars are labeled with dates and descriptions. They are someone's future pain, extracted and preserved.

## 3. Factions

**The Carnival Staff** operates the fairground with professional efficiency. The Ringmaster, who is addressed only as Host, manages the Wish Tent and conducts every transaction personally. Host is courteous, precise, and transparent about the terms: no customer is deceived about what they are trading. Host considers the Carnival a service. The wishes are real. The regrets are real. The exchange is voluntary. That the customers do not fully understand the cost is, in Host's view, a limitation of human imagination rather than a failure of disclosure.

**The Wish-Taken** are former customers living with the consequences. Some are satisfied: a woman who wished for courage and lost the regret of a future cowardice, leaving her permanently brave and permanently unable to learn from reckless choices. Others are disturbed: a man who wished for his daughter's recovery and lost the regret he would have felt for missing her childhood during a career he has not yet started. He does not know what he has lost. He knows something is missing. Support group facilitator Nell Archer has been collecting their stories and documenting the pattern.

**The Town Council** of the current host community manages the Carnival's presence. The Council did not invite the Carnival and cannot evict it (the field it occupies is unregistered land that does not appear on the town's property map). Councilwoman Iris Patel has proposed ignoring the Carnival until it leaves. She is overruled by the economic reality that the Carnival brings visitors, and visitors spend money.

**The Regret Seekers** are a small group of former customers who have returned to the Carnival's current location to reclaim what they traded. They want their regrets back. This is an unusual request, and Host has neither confirmed nor denied that returns are possible. The Seekers' most determined member, a teacher named Ewan Grove, traded the regret of a failed marriage that has not happened yet. He is now incapable of jealousy, which his wife has noticed and which is destroying their relationship more efficiently than the jealousy would have.

## 4. Unique Mechanic: Regret Ledger

Track removed regrets on a shared **Regret Ledger.**

**Making a wish:** The character states a wish. Host describes the price: a specific future regret, including when it would have occurred and what it would have taught. If the character accepts, the wish is granted and the regret is removed from their timeline.

**Effects of a removed regret:**
- The character cannot learn from mistakes in the removed regret's domain. Roll at -1d6 for related actions where growth or caution would normally apply.
- The character does not feel guilt, hesitation, or self-doubt about the related domain, which is an advantage in the short term and a liability over time.
- Related relationships are affected: people who expected the character to grow or change in response to the lost experience will notice the absence.

**Recovering a regret:** A character can recover a removed regret through:
- Confronting Host and negotiating a return (Presence roll, difficulty based on the regret's value)
- Living through the experience the regret was meant to teach, without the emotional preparation the regret would have provided
- Finding the regret's jar in the Collection Tent and breaking it (which returns the regret immediately and painfully)

**Recovered regrets** return as sudden, intense emotional experiences: the character feels the full weight of a sorrow they were supposed to process gradually. This is disorienting but restorative. After recovery, the -1d6 penalty is removed.

## 5. Character Archetypes

### The Regret Seeker

You traded the regret of a failed relationship for professional success. You got the success. Your marriage is falling apart because you are incapable of feeling the jealousy, fear, and vulnerability that would have taught you to fight for it. You have returned to the Carnival to get your regret back. You do not know whether Host will accept the return, and you do not know whether getting it back will save anything.

- **Drive:** *"Recover what I traded before the absence of it costs me more than the regret would have."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 2 | Mind 2 | Presence 3

### The Pattern Investigator

You have tracked the Pale Carnival across four towns over two years. You have documented 47 wishes and their consequences. You have not made a wish yourself, which the Carnival staff finds unusual. You are trying to understand the Carnival's purpose: where the regrets go, why the staff collects them, and what happens when the jars in the Collection Tent are full.

- **Drive:** *"Figure out what the Carnival is building with all those future sorrows."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 2 | Mind 3 | Presence 2

### The Town Councilwoman

You manage the town that the Carnival is currently occupying. You did not invite it. You cannot remove it. You are watching your neighbors visit the Wish Tent and return slightly different: happier, in a way that does not quite reach their eyes. You are a pragmatist and a protector, and you are trying to find a way to limit the Carnival's impact without provoking something you do not understand.

- **Drive:** *"Protect my community from a gift it does not know how to refuse."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 2 | Finesse 2 | Mind 2 | Presence 2

## 6. Starter Scenario & Why It Matters

**The Full Jar:** One of the Collection Tent's jars has cracked. The regret inside, belonging to a customer from a previous town, is leaking: residents near the fairground have begun experiencing sudden, intense sorrow about events that have not happened to them. A farmer wakes up crying about a divorce he has never had. A teenager feels devastated by a career failure she has not yet experienced. Host has announced that the Carnival will leave within 48 hours to "maintain containment." The Regret Seekers see this as their last chance to recover their trades. Nell Archer wants to enter the Collection Tent and document what is inside. Councilwoman Patel wants the Carnival gone immediately. Ewan Grove has been offered a deal by Host: he can have his regret back, but someone else must take his wish. The characters must navigate the cracking jar, the leaking sorrow, and the Carnival's departure, knowing that what they learn about the Collection Tent will determine whether the next town is warned or walks in blind.

**Why It Matters:** The Pale Carnival asks whether you would trade your future pain for a present benefit, and whether that trade is fair if you cannot understand what the pain was for. Regret is not just suffering. It is the mechanism through which people learn, change, and grow. A person without regret is not free. They are stuck: permanently the version of themselves that existed before the lesson they will never learn. The setting is about the cost of avoiding cost, and the specific cruelty of a system that gives people exactly what they ask for.
