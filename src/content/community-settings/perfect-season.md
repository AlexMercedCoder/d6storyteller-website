> *"We won 34-7 on Saturday. On Sunday, the Mercado family's restaurant burned down. We won 28-14 the week before. On Monday, old Mrs. Chen died. I have been keeping a list. Fifteen wins. Fifteen bad things. I showed the list to Coach Harlan. He told me I was imagining patterns. He did not look surprised."*
> - Jamie Oaks, Greyfield Wolves defensive midfielder

--

## 1. Setting Overview & Tone

Greyfield is a small city whose identity is wrapped around its sports team. The Wolves' winning streak has become a point of civic pride, generating revenue, media attention, and the kind of community energy that small cities rarely experience. Nobody wants the streak to end. Nobody wants to examine why the streak coincides with a growing list of community misfortunes that are individually explainable and collectively impossible to dismiss.

**Tone:** Anxious, investigative, and built on the specific dread of discovering that the thing your community loves most is the thing that is destroying it. The setting is a sports mystery where the question is not who committed the crime but who benefits from not asking.

**Sensory Pillars:**
1. **Sight:** Game day in Greyfield: banners, packed stands, and the particular energy of a community that is winning for the first time. The morning after: a boarded-up storefront, a moving truck, a memorial notice in the local paper. The list that Jamie Oaks keeps in a notebook, growing with each game.
2. **Sound:** The crowd during a game: jubilant, loud, carrying the sound of a community experiencing collective joy. The silence in the neighborhoods where the costs are paid: the quiet of a closed business, the absence of a person who was there last week.
3. **Touch & Smell:** The grass of the playing field, which has been healthier and greener than any groundskeeper can explain since the streak began. The weight of Jamie's notebook, which is a list of wins on one page and a list of losses on the other. The smell of smoke from the Mercado fire, still faintly present in the air three blocks away.

## 2. The City

**Greyfield Stadium** is the center of civic life during game season. The stadium is small (5,000 seats), old, and recently renovated with revenue from the winning streak.

**The Community Board** at City Hall tracks the misfortunes that nobody is connecting to the wins. The board lists business closures, departures, and deaths as separate events. Nobody has drawn lines between them.

**Coach Harlan's Office** in the stadium basement contains the team's records, Harlan's tactical notes, and a locked desk drawer that Jamie Oaks has noticed Harlan opens only on game days.

**The Old Grounds** are the original playing fields where Greyfield's first team played, now overgrown and abandoned. The Old Grounds contain a stone marker that is not a memorial to a player but an agreement, carved into stone, between the original team and something that the carving describes as "the patron of contest."

--

## 3. Factions

**The Coaching Staff** is led by Coach Dale Harlan, a veteran who has been coaching Greyfield for twelve years. Harlan knows about the connection between wins and costs. He has known since the streak began. He considers the arrangement a necessary evil: the team's success has revitalized the community, and the costs, while tragic, are distributed across the population in a way that no individual loss is large enough to draw investigation. He is wrong about the last part.

**The Players** are young athletes who are proud of their record and increasingly uneasy about the coincidences. Jamie Oaks is the most vocal, maintaining their list and pressing teammates to acknowledge the pattern. Most players do not want to look. The streak has brought them college scouts, media attention, and the kind of community status that small-city athletes dream about.

**The Community** of Greyfield is experiencing the best and worst period of its recent history simultaneously. The winning streak has brought investment, tourism, and pride. The cost has taken businesses, residents, and lives. Mayor Fern Aldric is aware of both trends and treats them as unrelated, which is either honest analysis or willful avoidance.

**The Patron** is the entity described in the Old Grounds marker. It is not a god, a demon, or a traditional supernatural being. It is a contractual force: something that guarantees victory in exchange for sacrifice, operating according to terms that Coach Harlan did not write and does not fully understand.

## 4. Unique Mechanic: Victory Cost

After every Wolves victory, roll on the **Victory Cost** table:

| Victory Margin | Cost Severity |
|---------------|--------------|
| 1-7 points (narrow win) | **Minor:** A small business closes. A public event is canceled. A longtime resident announces they are moving. |
| 8-20 points (comfortable win) | **Moderate:** A significant community asset is damaged (fire, flood, structural failure). A respected figure becomes seriously ill. |
| 21+ points (dominant win) | **Severe:** A death in the community. A permanent loss (school closure, landmark destruction). Multiple families depart. |

**Investigation:** Characters who investigate the pattern can reduce the cost by:
- Discovering the Old Grounds marker and reading the agreement (-1 severity)
- Confronting Coach Harlan and extracting the terms (-1 severity)
- Finding a way to modify or terminate the agreement (eliminates costs but may end the streak)

**Losing:** If the Wolves lose a game, no cost is incurred. But the question remains: does the community's misfortune stop, or does the Patron collect what it considers owed?

## 5. Character Archetypes

### The Investigating Player

You play defense and you keep a notebook. You have documented fifteen wins and fifteen misfortunes. You are not imagining the pattern. You are also a young athlete whose winning streak has brought college scouts and community recognition. Investigating the cost threatens the streak. The streak is your future. The cost is someone else's present.

- **Drive:** *"Prove the connection between wins and costs before the next victory kills someone I know, even if proving it ends the best thing that ever happened to my career."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 2 | Finesse 1 | Mind 3 | Presence 2

### The Coach Who Knows

You made the arrangement twelve years ago when the team was losing and the community was failing. You found the marker at the Old Grounds. You read the agreement. You accepted the terms. The winning streak has saved the community. The cost has damaged it. You consider the math acceptable. You are running out of the ability to make that calculation.

- **Drive:** *"Maintain the streak long enough for the community to stabilize without the Patron's help, then find a way to end the agreement before the costs exceed the benefits."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 2 | Mind 2 | Presence 3

### The Community Reporter

You cover local sports for the Greyfield Gazette. You have noticed the pattern between wins and community misfortunes. Your editor says it is a coincidence. Your data says otherwise. You are deciding whether to publish a story that would make you the person who ended Greyfield's perfect season and the community pride that comes with it.

- **Drive:** *"Publish the truth about the cost of winning, and accept that the community may blame the messenger rather than the message."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 2 | Mind 3 | Presence 2

## 6. Starter Scenario & Why It Matters

**Game Thirty-Four:** The Wolves are about to set the league's all-time winning record. The entire city is attending. College scouts are in the stands. The opposing team is weak, which means a dominant victory is likely, which means a severe cost. Jamie has shown their notebook to the community reporter. Coach Harlan has discovered that Jamie is investigating. The Old Grounds marker has been found by a local historian who is asking questions about the carving. The characters must decide whether to play for the record, investigate the cost, and confront the arrangement that has been sustaining the city's pride while eroding its foundation.

**Why It Matters:** The Perfect Season asks what a community is willing to sacrifice for a feeling of success. The Wolves' winning streak is real. The community pride is real. The cost is also real, and it is paid by people who did not agree to the arrangement. The setting is about collective bargains made without collective consent, and the specific moral problem of a system that distributes its benefits widely and its costs narrowly.
