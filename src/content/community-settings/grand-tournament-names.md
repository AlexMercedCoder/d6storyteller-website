## 1. Setting Overview & Tone

The Tournament of Names has governed Ashenmere for three centuries, replacing a civil war that was destroying the kingdom. The founders' logic was straightforward: if people are going to fight over power, let them fight over it directly, with clear rules, public witnesses, and consequences that are social rather than lethal. The system works. It is also deeply strange: a kingdom where a talented commoner can become a duke in an afternoon and a careless noble can lose everything in three minutes of combat.

**Tone:** Dramatic, politically charged, and built on the constant awareness that identity in Ashenmere is performative, contingent, and always at risk. The setting treats names as currency, reputation as armor, and the tournament as a legal system that is fair in principle and chaotic in practice.

**Sensory Pillars:**
1. **Sight:** The Tournament Court: a circular arena with viewing galleries, the combatants' names projected in luminous script above the fighting floor. The moment of transfer: when a name changes hands, the script above the loser dims and the winner's brightens, and the audience watches someone become someone else. The Nameless: people who have lost everything, who walk through the kingdom without legal identity, visible but unrecognized.
2. **Sound:** The Herald's announcement before each duel, reading the wagers in formal language that makes the exchange sound like a legal contract because it is one. The clash of weapons, which is real combat with blunted edges, painful and skillful but not lethal. The silence after a victory, when the crowd waits for the transfer to take effect and the loser's social world rearranges.
3. **Touch & Smell:** The weight of tournament weapons, which are heavier than combat weapons because they are designed for spectacle. The feel of a name being lost, which duelists describe as a cold emptiness where a certainty used to be, not painful but profoundly disorienting. The smell of the Tournament Court: sand, leather, and the sharp metallic scent that accompanies the name transfer enchantment.

## 2. The Kingdom

**The Tournament Court** is the kingdom's central arena: a permanent structure in the capital with seating for 5,000 and a fighting floor enchanted to register victory and transfer names. Lesser courts exist in every major town, handling local disputes.

**The Registry of Names** is the kingdom's record office, maintaining the official list of who holds which name, title, and social privilege. The Registrar, Warden Hale, updates the records after each tournament. He is meticulous, politically neutral, and the most important bureaucrat in the kingdom because his records determine who is legally who.

**The Nameless Quarter** is the district where people who have lost their names live. It is not a prison. It is a neighborhood of people who exist outside the kingdom's legal framework: they cannot own property, cannot sign contracts, and cannot be named in official documents. They survive through informal economy and mutual aid. The Quarter's organizer, a woman who goes by Shadow (she lost her name twelve years ago), maintains order and provides the structure that the legal system denies.

**The Training Halls** are where duelists prepare. Some are exclusive (Noble Hall trains aristocrats defending their titles), others are open (People's Arm trains commoners who want to fight their way up). The most successful trainer, a retired champion named Cole Briar, runs a hall that accepts anyone and charges based on results.

## 3. Factions

**The Nobility** defend their titles through the tournament because they have no other choice. Duchess Maren Ashford (the original, not the baker's daughter who took the name) lost her title to a commoner and has been petitioning the Crown to limit who can challenge whom. Her argument: the tournament was designed to settle disputes between equals, not to allow social revolution. Her opponents argue that equality before the tournament is the entire point.

**The Challengers** are commoners who use the tournament to climb. Their informal leader, a dock worker named Tomas Veer, has won four duels and now holds the title of Baron. He is a capable fighter, a poor administrator, and a symbol: proof that the tournament can elevate anyone, and a reminder that elevation does not come with training in governance.

**The Nameless** have no legal voice, which means they have no legal way to reclaim their identities. Shadow's organization operates outside the law because the law does not recognize their existence. The Nameless can challenge for names, but they must first find someone willing to be named as their opponent, which requires social connections that the Nameless by definition do not have.

**The Reform Movement** is led by Registrar Hale (quietly) and Cole Briar (loudly). They want three changes: a limit on how many names a single person can hold, a path to restoration for the Nameless that does not require combat, and oversight of the wagering system to prevent coerced challenges.

## 4. Unique Mechanic: Name Stakes

Before each duel, combatants declare wagers:

**Wager categories (from least to most valuable):**
| Wager | What Is Risked |
|-------|---------------|
| Privilege | A specific social right (property, office, membership) |
| Reputation | The public perception of the combatant (known for bravery, skill, honor) |
| Title | A noble title, professional rank, or official position |
| Family Name | The combatant's surname and all associated social connections |
| Given Name | The combatant's personal identity. Loss renders them Nameless. |

**Duel resolution:** The duel is resolved through standard D6 Storyteller contested rolls (Force for physical combat, Presence for verbal or social duels, Mind for strategic challenges). The winner claims the loser's wager. The transfer is immediate and legally binding.

**Reclaiming lost names:** A Nameless person can reclaim a name by:
- Challenging the person who holds it (requires someone to formally recognize the Nameless person as a valid challenger)
- Performing a service for the Crown significant enough to earn a new name
- Being adopted into a family that extends their name voluntarily

## 5. Character Archetypes

### The Rising Challenger

You were a baker's daughter. You are now Lady Ashford. You won the name in fair combat and the kingdom's records confirm it. You are also a baker's daughter who does not know how to manage an estate, navigate court politics, or respond to the original Lady Ashford's petition to have you stripped of the title. You won the duel. You are learning that winning the name was the easy part.

- **Drive:** *"Prove that I deserve the name I won, not by birthright but by what I do with it."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 3 | Finesse 1 | Mind 1 | Presence 3

### The Fallen Noble

You lost your family name in a duel you should have won. You are Nameless. You live in the Quarter. You are invisible to the legal system that defined your entire life. You are rebuilding: training, strategizing, and waiting for the opportunity to challenge for your name back. You are also discovering that the Nameless Quarter has a community, a culture, and a value system that the kingdom's name-based hierarchy does not recognize.

- **Drive:** *"Reclaim my name, or decide that the person I am becoming without it is worth more than the title I lost."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 2 | Finesse 2 | Mind 2 | Presence 2

### The Tournament Trainer

You have trained fifty duelists. You have seen commoners become nobles and nobles become Nameless. You run an open hall because you believe the tournament should be accessible to everyone, which is a principle that the nobility considers dangerous and the Challengers consider heroic. You fight the reform battle outside the arena because you have seen what happens inside it.

- **Drive:** *"Train fighters who understand that a name is responsibility, not property, and push the reforms through before the system's inequities destroy the tournament's legitimacy."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 2 | Finesse 1 | Mind 3 | Presence 2

## 6. Starter Scenario & Why It Matters

**The Three-Name Holder:** A merchant named Doriel Crane has won six consecutive duels and now holds three noble titles, two professional ranks, and the family names of four people who are now Nameless. Crane is within the tournament's rules. He is also concentrating social power in a way the system was designed to prevent. The Nameless Quarter is growing. Duchess Ashford (the original) is proposing a "Name Limit" law that would cap holdings at two. Crane is preparing to challenge Ashford for her remaining title. Cole Briar is training a Nameless fighter to challenge Crane, but the fighter needs a recognized name to issue a formal challenge, which creates a paradox: you need a name to fight for a name. The characters must navigate the legal, social, and physical dimensions of the tournament and decide whether the system needs reform, revolution, or defense.

**Why It Matters:** The Grand Tournament of Names asks who you are when your identity can be won or lost in three minutes. The tournament is a legal system that treats names as transferable property, which means identity in Ashenmere is earned, not given, and can be taken, not just diminished. The setting is about identity, social mobility, and the specific violence of a system that makes your place in the world contingent on your ability to defend it.
