## 1. Setting Overview & Tone

**High-Concept Pitch:** The djinn city is inside the human city, and the Gnawa are the diplomats.

Moroccan spirit tradition is layered and specific: the Jnoun (Moroccan plural of djinn) include the Mluk (spirit kings associated with specific colors and domains), the lesser djinn who occupy specific locations, and Aisha Qandisha, the wild and dangerous spirit of rivers and female power. The Gnawa tradition -- with its specific musical scales, colors, incenses, and ceremonies -- is a real spiritual and artistic tradition that works as a healing and diplomatic practice within this framework.

This setting situates the players in the Gnawa tradition as active practitioners, exploring a Morocco that acknowledges the Jnoun civilization fully and navigates it professionally.

**Sensory Pillars:**
1. **Sight:** The medinas and souks of a Moroccan city where Jnoun are present but invisible to ordinary sight -- in the shadow corner of the market, in the wall above the fountain, in the crowd that is slightly larger than it should be; the lila ceremony space with its color arrangements, the specific layout of offerings, and the distinctive way the candlelight behaves during successful callings.
2. **Sound:** The Gnawa music: the sintir's low bass drone, the krakebs' metallic rhythm, and the specific melodic patterns associated with each Mluk -- music that is also a calling, designed specifically to attract the attention of particular spirit types; the different sound quality of a space where Jnoun are present (subtle shifts that experienced practitioners recognize).
3. **Touch & Smell:** The specific incenses used in Jnoun work (each Mluk has associated scents), the texture of the ceremonial materials, the physical sensation of Jnoun presence reported by practitioners (warmth, cold, pressure, an awareness that is not one's own), and the smell of the Atlas cedar groves where the mountain Jnoun are strongest.

## 2. The Jnoun Courts

### The Seven Mluk
Seven spirit kings, each associated with a color, domain, and characteristic: the Black Sultan of the underworld and night, the White Mluk of the sea, the Red of conflict and passion, the Blue of sky and freedom, the Yellow of the desert, the Green of fertility and nature, and the mixed of threshold states. Each has courts, obligations, and relationships with the human world.

### The Gnawa Maalem Network
Gnawa master musicians across the region who maintain the ceremonial capacity: performing lilas for communities, training successors, and keeping the formal relationships with the Mluk active. They share information and support each other's work.

### Aisha Qandisha
The wild river spirit who is outside the Mluk system: older, more dangerous, and less predictable. She is not hostile by nature but she is not bound by the same relationship framework. Encounters with Aisha require different approaches.

### The Silent Court
The Mluk who has withdrawn has a court that is becoming destabilized. The lesser Jnoun who were part of that court are now unmanaged. The consequences are spreading through the human communities in the affected area.

## 3. Adventure Seeds

1. **The Withdrawn Mluk:** A specific Mluk has stopped responding to the lila ceremony. Community consequences (unexplained illness, relationship conflicts, business failures in the domain the Mluk governed) are multiplying. The Gnawa must discover why the Mluk withdrew and what would bring them back.

2. **The Jnoun Market:** The players must attend a Jnoun market (accessible in the period between midnight and dawn at a specific location) to obtain information or a specific item needed to address the current crisis. Navigating the market requires knowing the customs -- and the Jnoun custom around human visitors is complicated.

3. **The New Spirit:** Something has arrived in the city that is not part of the established Jnoun civilization: a spirit from outside the tradition, whose nature the Gnawa framework does not cover. Determining what it is, what it wants, and how to address it without disturbing the existing relationships requires research and improvisation.

4. **The Community Crisis:** A community member has a significant, involuntary relationship with a specific Jnoun -- the Jnoun has attached to them and the attachment is becoming dangerous. The lila ceremony can address it, but the person in question does not believe in the tradition. Navigating consent, cultural difference, and supernatural reality requires care.

5. **The Lineage:** An old Maalem is dying without a trained successor. The Gnawa tradition requires a specific type of initiation that cannot be rushed. The Mluk relationships this Maalem holds will not automatically transfer. If they cannot be transferred before the Maalem dies, those relationships must be rebuilt from the beginning at a time when the Jnoun situation is already precarious.
