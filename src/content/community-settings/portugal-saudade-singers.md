> *"Saudade is not sadness. Sadness is about what you have lost. Saudade is about what you never had-the future that did not happen, the love that was not spoken, the ship that did not return. When I sing fado, I give voice to that absence. And sometimes, the absence answers."*
> - Maria da Saudade, Fadista of Alfama, 1780

## 1. Setting Overview & Tone

**High-Concept Pitch:** **Saudade** is the most important word in Portuguese culture-an untranslatable emotional state that combines melancholy, longing, nostalgia, and the bittersweet awareness of absence. **Fado** is saudade made music: the mournful, powerful vocal tradition of Lisbon's working-class neighborhoods. In this setting, saudade is not merely an emotion but a supernatural force, and fado is not merely music but spirit-summoning.

Set in the decades following the **1755 Lisbon earthquake** (which destroyed 85% of the city and killed an estimated 30,000-50,000 people), Portugal is a nation defined by loss. The earthquake shattered both the physical city and the philosophical certainty of the Enlightenment. Into this void of meaning, the fado singers of the Alfama (the oldest neighborhood, which survived the earthquake) discover that their songs of saudade can pierce the boundary between the living and the dead, summoning the ghosts of sailors lost at sea, the spirits trapped beneath the earthquake rubble, and the **Moura Encantada**-enchanted Moorish women of Portuguese legend who were sealed into the land during the Reconquista and still wait to be freed.

**Sensory Pillars:**
1. **Sight:** Lisbon's seven hills rebuilt in Pombaline architectural style after the earthquake, its new grid streets contrasting with the ancient, organic tangle of Alfama; spectral caravels materializing in the fog on the Tagus River, their ghostly crews still navigating routes to India and Brazil; the shimmering, half-visible form of a Moura Encantada trapped in a fountain or castle ruin.
2. **Sound:** The raw, heartbreaking voice of a fadista singing in a tiny Alfama tavern, accompanied by the Portuguese guitar's distinctive metallic shimmer; the whispered prayers of earthquake ghosts still trapped under rubble 25 years after their deaths; the roar of the Atlantic beyond the river mouth.
3. **Touch & Smell:** Salt air from the Tagus mixed with the scent of grilled sardines and port wine; the cool stone of earthquake-damaged walls; the warm vibration of the Portuguese guitar against the body.

## 2-3. Historical Context & Factions

The 1755 Lisbon earthquake was one of the deadliest natural disasters in European history and profoundly impacted Enlightenment philosophy (Voltaire's *Candide* was partly a response). Portuguese folklore features the **Moura Encantada**-enchanted Moorish maidens sealed into fountains, bridges, and castles, associated with hidden treasure and the melancholy of lost Iberian Al-Andalus.

**Factions:** The Fadistas of Alfama (spirit-singers), the Earthquake Ghosts (the trapped dead beneath Lisbon), and the Moura Encantada (ancient enchanted beings seeking release).

## 4. Unique Mechanic: Saudade (The Longing)

- **1-2 (Present):** Firmly in the living world. +1d6 to Force. Cannot perceive ghosts.
- **3-4 (Longing):** Can perceive ghosts and Moura Encantada. Fado singing can summon specific spirits. +1d6 to Presence. **Risk:** The character begins to live more in memory than in the present.
- **5-6 (Lost in Saudade):** Can summon ghost fleets, communicate with the earthquake dead, and free Moura Encantada. +1d6 to all rolls involving the supernatural. **Risk:** The character is fading from the present. At Saudade 6, they must perform an act of radical present-tense joy (falling in love, creating something new, laughing uncontrollably) or become a ghost themselves.

## 5. Character Archetypes

### Archetype 1: The Fadista of Alfama
- **Quote:** *"My voice called my dead husband back from the sea. He stood in the doorway dripping seawater and asked me to sing another verse. I sang until dawn. When the sun came up, he was gone. That is saudade."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 1 | Mind 2 | Presence 4

### Archetype 2: The Earthquake Archaeologist
- **Quote:** *"I dig in the rubble and I find bones, gold, and ghosts. The bones I catalog. The gold I return. The ghosts I listen to."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 2 | Finesse 2 | Mind 3 | Presence 1

### Archetype 3: The Navigator of Ghost Ships
- **Quote:** *"My great-grandfather's caravel never returned from India. Last Tuesday, it appeared in the fog on the Tagus. He was still at the helm. He asked me for directions home."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 2 | Finesse 3 | Mind 2 | Presence 1

## 6-7. Conditions & Starter Scenario

**Conditions include:** *Saudade Paralysis* (longing so intense it prevents action), *Ghost Touch* (cold from spectral contact), *Earthquake Echo* (reliving the 1755 quake), *Moura's Kiss* (enchanted by a Moura Encantada), *Voice Spent* (fado singing has strained the spirit along with the vocal cords).

**Starter Scenario: The Ghost Fleet.** A fleet of spectral caravels has appeared in the Tagus, blocking the harbor. The ghost ships are the lost fleet of King Sebastian I (who vanished at the Battle of Alcazar in 1578). The Fadista must sing them to rest. The Archaeologist has found the earthquake-buried chapel where Sebastian's cenotaph was sealed. The Navigator must board the ghost flagship and convince the phantom king that the empire he sought to save is already gone. The catch: the Portuguese people still believe Sebastian will return (Sebastianism). Laying his ghost to rest means killing a national hope.

## 8. Why This Setting Matters

Portugal's cultural identity is built on saudade-on longing for what is absent. This makes it the most emotionally sophisticated mythology for a tabletop RPG setting. The Saudade mechanic forces players to engage with the paradox at the heart of all storytelling: we create fiction because we long for something reality does not provide. The fadista who sings the dead back to life is doing exactly what every RPG player does at the table: using narrative to make absence present, if only for a few hours.
