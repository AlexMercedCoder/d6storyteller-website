## 1. Setting Overview & Tone

The characters move between the opera house and the kingdom beneath it. Above, they may be performers, technicians, critics, patrons, or people who have discovered a secret no one is meant to know. Below, every costume is a station and every dramatic convention becomes a physical law. A character who takes the role of a villain may command fear in the underworld, but the role will demand a proper ending.

The relationship between the worlds is literal and immediate. A tragedy staged above creates suffering below. A comedy lifts spirits and loosens authority. A love story can forge alliances between underground houses that have been feuding for generations. The people below know this. The people above, mostly, do not.

**Tone:** Lush backstage intrigue with consequences that refuse to stay metaphorical. The setting is playful about theatrical convention, but it takes artists, craftspeople, and the people affected by public stories seriously. There is glamour here, and there is also the particular exhaustion of people who work behind the curtain while someone else takes the bow.

**Sensory Pillars:**
1. **Sight:** The opera house interior: gilt balconies, velvet seats, and a stage whose boards are worn smooth by decades of performers. Below, the Painted Capital: streets that look like stage sets because they are, buildings backed by canvas flats, and a sky that is always the color of the last production's final scene. The border between worlds: a trapdoor, a fly-loft rope, a mirror that reflects the wrong room.
2. **Sound:** The orchestra tuning, which the people below hear as weather changing. Applause, which sounds like thunder underground. The creak of rigging in the fly loft, where every rope connects to something below that the crew cannot see but can feel when it pulls back. The voice of an understudy rehearsing a part they will never perform above, knowing that the rehearsal is already writing policy below.
3. **Touch & Smell:** Greasepaint, which smells like wax and mineral spirits and clings to skin for hours. The rough texture of hemp rope in the fly loft, fraying at the joints where the worlds meet. The cold draft from beneath the stage, which carries the smell of paint, dust, and something older: the accumulated scent of every set that was struck and stored and never entirely disappeared.

## 2. Two Worlds, One Season

**The Fly Loft** is the thin place between worlds. Ropes, counterweights, and discarded props form a vertical maze whose exits change after every rehearsal. The stage crew uses it to smuggle food and messages below, which is why the theatre's management keeps trying to replace them with automated rigging. The automation never works correctly. The ropes have opinions.

**The Painted Capital** lies beneath the main stage. Its buildings are backed by scenery, its streets rearrange when the set changes, and its ruling houses owe allegiance to the roles they embody. A new comedy can turn a feared minister into an object of ridicule overnight. The Capital's population is roughly 3,000, and they have their own culture, families, and traditions built around centuries of productions.

**The Prompt Archive** stores scripts from every production ever staged. The pages are dangerous because an unperformed scene may still become real below if enough people believe it ought to have happened. The Archivist, a woman named Corvid, guards the collection and reads voraciously. She has read every script in the collection and knows which unperformed scenes could change the kingdom below. She does not share this information freely.

**The Green Room** is a neutral space between worlds where people from above and below can meet without their roles enforcing behavior. It exists behind a particular mirror in the opera house's dressing rooms and can be accessed only by people who know it is there. The Green Room smells like old coffee and costume fabric. It is the only place where a duke from below and a stagehand from above can sit at the same table without the story telling them how to behave.

## 3. Factions

**The Royal Company** wants a profitable season and treats the hidden kingdom as a technical nuisance. Its artistic director, Madame Leveaux, knows enough to exploit applause when it is useful and deny responsibility when it is not. She is planning a season of revolutionary plays because revolution sells tickets. She has not considered what revolution means for the people living inside the scenery.

**The Court of Understudies** represents people beneath who were assigned lesser roles by old productions. They want the coming revolutionary opera to change the casting order, even if the change costs lives above. Their leader, a man called the Second King (because no production has ever given him the lead), has spent twenty years preparing for a script that lets him take the stage. He is willing to wait for the right play. He is less willing to wait through another season of comedies.

**The Stagehands' Union** knows the crossing points and has the power to halt a production. It is divided between protecting members from danger and exposing the theatre's dependence on their uncredited work. Shop steward Pim holds the key to the fly loft and considers the Union's silence about the kingdom below a form of complicity. She has not yet decided what to do about it because breaking the silence would also break the Union's leverage.

**The Critics' Circle** reviews every production for the city's newspapers. Most critics do not know about the kingdom below. One does: a retired reviewer named Gault, who discovered the connection decades ago and has been writing coded reviews ever since, warning the people below when a dangerous production is coming. Gault's reviews are famous for their obscure metaphors. The people below read them as prophecy.

## 4. Unique Mechanic: Applause Power

Start a production with **Applause Power** equal to 3.

**Gaining Power (+1 each):**
- Create a moment the audience sincerely responds to
- Reveal a truth that changes the production's meaning
- Protect the workers making the show possible
- Perform a scene that serves the people below rather than exploiting them

**Spending Power:**
- 1 Power: Change a local scene below (rearrange a street, open a door, shift a mood)
- 1 Power: Command attention from a role-bound figure below
- 2 Power: Give a character temporary authority consistent with their theatrical role

**Backlash:** When a scene falls flat, the audience turns hostile, or the production relies on cruelty for effect, mark Backlash. At 3 Backlash, an underworld rival gains a dramatic advantage: a door closes at the wrong moment, a secret reaches the wrong listener, or a role refuses its scheduled conclusion. Clear Backlash by changing the production's actual conditions, not by simply putting on a better show. The kingdom below can tell the difference between genuine change and a better performance of the same story.

## 5. Character Archetypes

### The Assistant Stage Manager

You can call every cue in the building and know which one opened a doorway last week. You have been managing crossings for three years, keeping a production running above while monitoring its effects below. You are tired. You are also the only person in the building who sees both sides of every scene change.

- **Drive:** *"Keep the company alive long enough to make the people above admit what their stories cost below."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 3 | Mind 2 | Presence 2

### The Understudy Envoy

You came up from the Painted Capital wearing a borrowed face. If the curtain falls while you are here, you may not keep it. You are negotiating on behalf of the Second King for a production that gives your people better roles. You understand theatrical politics from a perspective that no one above has considered: what it means to live inside a story someone else is telling.

- **Drive:** *"Win a role for my people that does not require anyone else to become a villain."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 2 | Finesse 2 | Mind 1 | Presence 3

### The Retired Critic

You stopped reviewing five years ago, but your column still runs with reprints. Below, your old reviews are constitutional documents. You know more about the connection between the worlds than anyone alive, and you have been protecting the people below through careful language for decades. You are getting old. You need someone to continue the work, and you are not sure anyone above will believe you long enough to learn.

- **Drive:** *"Find someone who can read between the lines before I am no longer here to write them."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 1 | Mind 3 | Presence 3

## 6. Starter Scenario & Why It Matters

**Opening Night:** The lead actor vanishes twelve hours before a premiere. Below, the role the actor was set to play has selected a real monarch to die in its place: the revolutionary script calls for an execution in the final act, and the kingdom's ruling house knows that what happens on stage becomes their reality. The theatre wants a replacement onstage before the audience notices the change. The Court of Understudies wants the show performed exactly as written, because the revolution in the script is their best chance at rewriting the hierarchy below. The Stagehands' Union is threatening to strike unless the company acknowledges what the production will cost. The characters have one performance to find the actor, change the ending, negotiate with the people below, and decide whether the revolution the audience wants to see is one the kingdom below can survive.

**Why It Matters:** The Kingdom Beneath the Theater asks who controls the stories that shape other people's lives. The setting is about representation in both senses: theatrical and political. The people below live inside narratives they did not write, performed by people who do not know they have an audience. The characters must decide whether to change the story, change the audience, or change the relationship between the two. There is no option that does not cost someone their role.
