## 1. Setting Overview & Tone

Candlemark awards degrees in disciplines that can break a city by accident. Its houses compete for grants and prestige while a sealed observatory begins predicting faculty deaths. Students are the first to notice because nobody powerful takes their questions seriously.

The university is not a school detached from the world. Candlemark's graduates certify bridges, negotiate with weather, advise courts, and teach magic to the children of wealthy families. Its mistakes become public disasters. This makes the campus a place where tuition, research funding, and family connections determine who gets forgiven for a dangerous experiment and who gets expelled for the same one.

**Tone:** Curious, social, and a little claustrophobic. Students have time to make friends, attend lectures, and be ridiculous together. They also have less power than the people who expect them to solve every inconvenient mystery quietly. The setting is a campus story first: friendships, rivalries, late nights, and shared meals. The magic makes the stakes higher, but the social dynamics are the ones anyone who has attended a school will recognize.

**Sensory Pillars:**
1. **Sight:** Candlelit lecture halls with charts and diagrams that move on their own, illustrating principles the professor has not yet explained. The Sealed Observatory dome cracking open against a night sky, light spilling upward instead of down. Students carrying stacks of books that occasionally rearrange themselves based on reading priority. The campus at dusk, when the safety wards along the pathways glow amber and the gargoyles on the older buildings turn their heads to watch who is out late.
2. **Sound:** The scratch of a quill during an exam, in a room where writing the wrong answer produces a faint hiss from the paper. The low hum of the ward boundary around the Restricted Wing, which sounds like a sustained note from a cello and intensifies when someone unauthorized approaches. The bell that signals the end of term, which rings differently each year, in a pitch that corresponds to the collective mood of the graduating class.
3. **Touch & Smell:** The particular weight of a Candlemark textbook, which is heavier when it contains information the reader is not yet authorized to understand. The cold stone of the Night Library staircase, worn smooth by generations of students visiting after hours. The smell of the campus: old paper, candle wax, wet wool, and the sharp ozone scent that lingers after a practical exam goes wrong.

## 2. Campus Life

**The House Courts** divide students by patronage rather than aptitude. Each House offers resources and introductions, then expects loyalty when faculty disputes become public. A student may transfer Houses, but only at the cost of becoming someone else's betrayal story. The four Houses are named for their founding patrons: Ardent (military and governance), Veil (espionage and diplomacy), Crucible (engineering and construction), and Lantern (healing and agriculture).

**The Night Library** contains books removed from official reading lists. Its librarians allow access in exchange for favors that are scholarly, inconvenient, and usually fair. The Library occupies three underground floors and is older than the university itself. Its head librarian, a man called Sable, has worked there for longer than any living faculty member can verify.

**The Sealed Observatory** has been closed since a telescope predicted the death of its founder, forty years ago. Its dome has begun opening by itself after midnight, and the prediction ledger now includes students. The Faculty Senate has assigned a junior professor to investigate. The junior professor has not visited the Observatory once. The students have visited it eleven times.

**The Commons** is the social heart of campus: a large hall with a fire pit, mismatched furniture, and a wall where students post anonymous questions, confessions, and warnings. The Commons stays open all night. Its unofficial rule is that nothing said at the fire pit after midnight can be used as evidence by any House or faculty body. This rule is honored more often than not.

## 3. Factions

**The Faculty Senate** protects Candlemark's reputation and calls most student concerns a matter for internal discipline. Its chair, Professor Delaine, is a competent administrator who believes the university's public image is its most valuable asset. Some senators are covering up research abuse. Others are simply frightened of losing the grants that keep their departments alive. Delaine suspects both and is unsure which frightens her more.

**The House Prefects** run student life and know nearly every secret worth knowing. They can organize help faster than the Senate, but their authority depends on keeping scandal out of the newspapers. The current Ardent Prefect, a final-year student named Jorin Hale, has been investigating the Observatory independently and has found something he has not shared with the other Prefects.

**The Night Library Circle** believes dangerous knowledge should be taught with care instead of locked away. Its members are not united on what care means when an experiment could harm a whole city. The Circle's most vocal member, a third-year named Tessa Vane, recently accessed a restricted text that described the Observatory telescope's original design, and the design includes a component that is not in any official schematic.

**The Alumni Board** funds the university and expects results. Its members are Candlemark graduates who hold positions of power in the outside world: judges, architects, military advisors, and court mages. The Board's interest in the Observatory is more urgent than the Senate realizes. Two Board members have received predictions. They have not disclosed this to the Senate. They are sending their own investigator.

## 4. Unique Mechanic: Academic Pressure

Each character begins a term with **Academic Pressure 0.** Mark +1 when a deadline, faculty scrutiny, financial problem, risky research choice, or House obligation narrows the character's options.

**Spending Pressure:** A character may spend 1 Pressure before a roll to declare a prepared fact, use an overlooked resource, or gain +1d6 through intense study. The pressure is real, but so is the work it represents.

**Thresholds:**
- **At 3 Pressure:** Choose a visible consequence: missed sleep (-1d6 on physical actions until rest), an angry mentor (social complication), a House obligation (owe a favor), or a rumor that changes how people treat you.
- **At 5 Pressure:** The university takes formal notice: probation, a public hearing, or loss of access to a vital location. The character's situation becomes part of the campus conversation.

**Clearing Pressure:** Rest (1 session of downtime). Honest support from another character (-1). Completing work on fair terms (-1). Refusing an obligation that would make success hollow (-2, but may create a new enemy). Pressure does not clear through avoidance or denial. The university notices when students disappear.

## 5. Character Archetypes

### The Scholarship Student

You are excellent at the work and one unpaid bill away from losing access to it. Your scholarship requires maintaining top marks, contributing to a faculty research project, and representing your House at public events. You do all three. You also tutor three students who can afford to pay, because the scholarship does not cover textbooks.

- **Drive:** *"Prove I belong here without becoming grateful for every unfair condition they attach to that proof."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 2 | Mind 3 | Presence 2

### The Prefect's Assistant

You know how the campus works and how much trouble a clean public story can hide. You handle scheduling, complaints, and the quiet conversations that happen when a student's problem is too sensitive for a formal report. You are good at keeping things running. You are beginning to wonder whether keeping things running is the same as keeping them right.

- **Drive:** *"Keep students safe without becoming the person who asks them to stay quiet."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 2 | Mind 2 | Presence 3

### The Night Library Regular

You spend more time underground than in class. Sable knows your name. You have read texts that the Faculty Senate does not know exist, and you have started noticing connections between the Observatory predictions and a research project that was canceled twenty years ago. Your academic standing is precarious because you prioritize the wrong readings. You consider them the right ones.

- **Drive:** *"Follow the research wherever it leads, even if it leads to something the university cannot survive knowing."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 1 | Mind 4 | Presence 2

## 6. Starter Scenario & Why It Matters

**The Extra Exam:** Every student in Candlemark receives a test paper for a course that does not exist. The questions describe a ritual scheduled for tomorrow, including the name of a student who will be harmed if it succeeds. The handwriting on the test matches the Observatory's prediction ledger. The Faculty Senate calls the papers a prank and orders them collected. The House Prefects are dividing into those who want to investigate and those who want the incident to disappear quietly. The Alumni Board's investigator arrives on campus tonight. The characters must find who has access to the Observatory, determine what the test is actually testing, decide whether taking the exam is participation or investigation, and stop the ritual without giving the administration an excuse to erase the evidence.

**Why It Matters:** Candlemark University asks who gets to decide what is too dangerous to know. The university is built on the assumption that knowledge is controlled by qualifying the people who access it: exams, certifications, tenure. The Observatory breaks that assumption by producing knowledge that selects its own audience. The characters are students, which means they occupy the position of people who are told they are not ready. The setting asks whether readiness is earned through study or claimed through action, and whether the institution that teaches you has the right to decide what you are allowed to learn.
