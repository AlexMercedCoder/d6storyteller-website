## 1. Setting Overview & Tone

**High-Concept Pitch:** The Argentine **pampas** are among the largest grasslands on earth--a sea of grass stretching from Buenos Aires province to the Patagonian steppe, broken only by isolated estancias, lonely ombú trees, and the distant spine of the Andes. In the 19th century, the **gaucho**--the nomadic horseback culture of the pampas--represented freedom, fierce independence, and a deeply fatalistic relationship with the land and death. Argentine folklore is saturated with supernatural encounters born from this landscape: the **Luz Mala** (evil light--a spectral glow that appears over the pampas at night, marking sites of buried bones and restless spirits), the **Mandinga** (a devil figure who appears at crossroads offering bargains), the **Lobizón** (the Argentine werewolf--the seventh consecutive son who transforms under the full moon), and the **Llorona** (the weeping woman spirit of the river valleys).

In this setting, the pampas themselves are a character. The sheer scale of emptiness works on the human psyche, thinning the boundary between the living and the dead. Gauchos who ride alone for too long begin to see the spectral riders--ghosts of the fallen from centuries of warfare, colonial violence, and Indigenous displacement--who still traverse the grasslands on phantom horses, endlessly searching for home.

**Sensory Pillars:**
1. **Sight:** The pampas stretching to every horizon in an unbroken line of silver-green grass that shimmers like water in the heat; a single ombú tree standing alone in miles of grassland, its ancient trunk twisted by centuries of wind, a gaucho's horse tied to its roots; the sickly yellowish-green glow of a Luz Mala hovering two feet above the grass in the pre-dawn darkness, pulsing like a heartbeat; a column of ghostly riders visible only at the extreme edge of vision, always the same distance away no matter how fast you ride toward them.
2. **Sound:** The profound silence of the pampas broken only by wind through tall grass--a sound like breathing; the lonely, haunting rhythm of a gaucho's guitar (*milonga*) played at a campfire that seems to be the only light in the universe; the distant howl of a Lobizón that sounds almost but not quite like a dog; the whispered crying of the Llorona carried on river mist that makes you want to walk toward it.
3. **Touch & Smell:** The bone-deep cold of the pampa wind (*pampero*) that sweeps up from Patagonia without warning, dropping temperatures by twenty degrees in minutes; the comforting weight of a facón (gaucho knife) in a leather sheath against the hip; the rich, smoky scent of *asado* (open-fire grilled beef) that is the social anchor of gaucho life; the electric tingling of Luz Mala proximity that raises every hair on the body.

**The Core Tension:** Freedom vs. Belonging. The gaucho tradition prizes absolute independence--the open sky, the lone rider, no master. But the supernatural threats of the pampas prey specifically on isolation. The ghosts target those who are alone. The Lobizón transforms in solitude. The Luz Mala leads solitary travelers to unmarked graves. To survive, the gaucho must find community without surrendering the freedom that defines them.

## 2. Historical & Mythological Context

The gaucho is the cultural icon of Argentina, comparable to the cowboy in the United States but carrying deeper philosophical and literary significance. The gaucho tradition emerged from the mixing of Spanish colonial, Indigenous (Mapuche, Tehuelche, Querandí), and African cultures on the vast grasslands.

Argentine folklore is one of the richest supernatural traditions in South America. The **Luz Mala** ("evil light" or "bad light") is a real phenomenon (likely bioluminescence from decomposing organic matter or will-o'-the-wisps) that has been documented across the pampas for centuries, always associated with sites of burial or violence. The **Lobizón** (from the Portuguese *lobisomem*) is the Argentine/Uruguayan werewolf variant, traditionally the seventh consecutive son, who transforms on Friday nights during full moons. The **Mandinga** is a devil figure at crossroads (related to the broader Latin American tradition of the diabolical bargain). The **Llorona** (weeping woman) appears near rivers and waterways.

**Mapuche** culture contributes a profound spiritual dimension. The Mapuche nation (spanning southern Argentina and Chile) maintains a rich cosmological system involving *machi* (spiritual healers who are often gender-diverse), *wekufe* (malevolent spirits), and the *rewe* (sacred ceremonial post that connects the human world to the spirit world).

> **Note on Respectful Portrayal:** Gaucho culture, Mapuche spirituality, and Argentine folklore are living traditions with deep significance to the people of Argentina. The historical displacement of Indigenous peoples during the "Conquest of the Desert" campaign is acknowledged as a trauma that echoes through the supernatural landscape of this setting.

## 3. Core Factions & Traditions

### The Paisanos del Crepúsculo (Companions of Twilight)
A loose brotherhood of gaucho mystics, wandering healers, and supernatural troubleshooters who ride the pampas addressing spiritual afflictions and hunting dangerous entities.
- **Cultural Association:** Gauchos who have encountered the supernatural and survived, binding together in an informal network connected by shared campfires, coded milonga songs, and a mutual understanding that the pampas are haunted. They include former military deserters, payadores (improvising poet-singers), and curanderas (folk healers).
- **Descriptor Examples:**
  - *"I have ridden the pampas alone for ten years. The ghosts keep me company. Most of them are polite."*
  - *"My facón was forged from a cavalry saber broken at the Battle of Caseros. It cuts spiritual flesh as well as physical."*
  - *"I can read the Luz Mala like a map. Each color tells me what kind of death happened there."*

### The Mapuche Machi (Spirit Walkers of the Cordillera)
Mapuche spiritual healers who cross between the human world and the spirit world through the high Andes passes.
- **Cultural Association:** The machi is the most important spiritual role in Mapuche society--healer, prophet, and defender against *wekufe*. Machi traditionally undergo a profound spiritual calling (*küymi*) that includes visions, illness, and gender transformation. They use the sacred *kultrun* drum to enter trance and communicate with spirits.
- **Descriptor Examples:**
  - *"My kultrun drum was carved from the wood of a tree struck by lightning. It opens doors that no hand can touch."*
  - *"I walk between worlds through the Andes passes where the wind carries spirits. The higher you climb, the thinner the veil."*
  - *"The wekufe fear the sound of my voice because I know their names."*

### The Ghost Riders (Jinetes Fantasma)
Not a faction the players join but the omnipresent supernatural threat and occasional ally of the pampas--the spectral horseback riders who endlessly traverse the grasslands.
- **Cultural Association:** The accumulated ghosts of centuries of violence on the pampas: colonial soldiers, Indigenous warriors, gaucho outlaws, and victims of the Conquest of the Desert. They are not necessarily hostile but are deeply confused, trapped in loops of memory, endlessly riding toward destinations that no longer exist.
- **Descriptor Examples (for characters who can communicate with them):**
  - *"The ghost rider told me he was going home. His home has been a wheat field for eighty years."*
  - *"If you match their pace and ride alongside them, they sometimes remember they are dead. That moment of clarity is the most heartbreaking thing you will ever witness."*

## 4. Unique Mechanic: Soledad (Solitude)

Characters track a **Soledad Meter** (1-6):

- **1-2 (Grounded / Con Gente):** The character is firmly rooted in the living world through strong social bonds. +1d6 to Presence when interacting with living humans. Completely invisible to ghosts, Luz Mala, and spiritual entities. Safe, but unable to perceive or combat supernatural threats.
- **3-4 (Liminal / Entre Mundos):** The character exists between the living and the dead. They can see ghosts, read Luz Mala, and perceive the spiritual landscape of the pampas. +1d6 to Mind when investigating supernatural phenomena. They can communicate with spirits by riding alongside them and matching pace. Costs 1 Momentum per scene. **Risk:** The character casts a faint shadow even at noon, and animals become uneasy around them.
- **5-6 (Ghostward / Casi Muerto):** The character is more spirit than flesh. +1d6 to Force and Finesse in spiritual combat. They can enter the spectral pampas (the ghost version of the grasslands where the dead ride), physically interact with spirits, and wield Luz Mala energy as a weapon. **Risk:** The character is becoming invisible to the living. Friends and strangers alike fail to notice them. Food provides no satisfaction. They leave no footprints. At Soledad 6, the character has one session to restore a meaningful human connection or they join the Ghost Riders permanently.

**Adjusting Soledad:** Every day spent riding alone raises Soledad by 1. Every scene of genuine human connection--sharing a meal, playing music together, embracing someone, telling someone your real name--lowers it by 1. The *asado* (communal barbecue) is the single most effective Soledad remedy: a full asado with friends lowers Soledad by 2.

## 5. Character Archetypes

### Archetype 1: The Payador (Gaucho Poet-Singer)
- **Concept:** A wandering poet-singer who improvises verse (*payadas*) at estancia gatherings. His songs are so emotionally true that they resonate with the dead as well as the living. He discovered this when his *milonga* about a fallen soldier caused the ghost of that soldier to materialize at the campfire, weeping and singing along. Now he uses his gift deliberately--singing spirits to rest, challenging the Mandinga to duels of verse, and composing songs so beautiful they temporarily repair the boundary between worlds.
- **Quote:** *"The dead miss music more than anything. They miss the vibration. A good song gives a ghost permission to feel something again, and sometimes that is enough for them to let go."*
- **Suggested Descriptors:** *"My guitar strings are tuned to a frequency that ghosts can hear"*, *"Won a verse-duel against the Mandinga at a crossroads and lived to rhyme about it"*, *"Can improvise a ten-verse payada on any topic, including topics that don't exist yet"*, *"The ghosts request specific songs now. They have favorites."*
- **Drive:** *"Sing every restless spirit on the pampas to peace, one song at a time."*
- **Flaw:** *"I am so attuned to the emotional needs of the dead that I neglect the living people who love me."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 1 | Mind 2 | Presence 4

### Archetype 2: The Machi of the Southern Wind
- **Concept:** A Mapuche machi who has crossed the Andes from Chile into the Argentine pampas following a vision. The vision showed a great spiritual wound on the grasslands--a scar left by the Conquest of the Desert--that is leaking wekufe (malevolent spirits) into the human world. She carries her kultrun drum, speaks to the wind, and must navigate a land that has been stripped of the Indigenous sacred sites that would normally serve as spiritual anchors.
- **Quote:** *"This land is sick. Not the soil--the soil is rich. The spirit of the land is sick. Someone cut its roots, and now the wound is infected with wekufe."*
- **Suggested Descriptors:** *"Machi of the Pewenche Mapuche, called by the spirits at age twelve through a fever that lasted forty days"*, *"My kultrun drum's rhythm can open doors between worlds at the high mountain passes"*, *"I see the spiritual landscape overlaid on the physical one--the pampas should be shining, but I see only scars"*, *"The wekufe fear me because I know the songs that bind them"*.
- **Drive:** *"Heal the spiritual wound on the pampas before the wekufe infection spreads beyond containment."*
- **Flaw:** *"I am a foreigner in this land, and the gaucho culture does not easily trust Mapuche authority."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 2 | Mind 3 | Presence 2

### Archetype 3: The Seventh Son (Lobizón)
- **Concept:** The seventh consecutive son of a gaucho family, carrying the Lobizón curse. Every Friday night during the full moon, he transforms into a massive, wolf-like beast that runs the pampas until dawn. Unlike the folklore suggests, the transformation is not complete madness--he retains fragments of his human mind, enough to avoid his family's estancia but not enough to control the hunger. He rides with the Paisanos del Crepúsculo because they are the only people who know what he is and don't try to kill him for it.
- **Quote:** *"My father had seven sons because he wanted help with the cattle. He got six ranch hands and one monster. He loves all seven of us equally. That is why I have not put a silver bullet in my own chest."*
- **Suggested Descriptors:** *"Transform into a Lobizón every full-moon Friday, retaining fragmented human awareness"*, *"Superhuman senses of smell and hearing even in human form"*, *"The strongest gaucho on the pampas--I can wrestle a bull to the ground bare-handed"*, *"I carry silver bullets for the day I lose control completely"*.
- **Drive:** *"Find a way to control the transformation, or at least ensure that when I lose control, no innocent person is within range."*
- **Flaw:** *"The closer the full moon, the shorter my temper, the stronger my appetites, and the less I trust my own judgment."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 4 | Finesse 2 | Mind 1 | Presence 1

## 6. Condition Palette

**Physical Conditions:**
- **Minor:** *Pampero Chill* (bone-deep cold from the Patagonian wind), *Saddle Worn* (raw from days of riding), *Luz Mala Headache* (proximity to evil light causes migraines).
- **Moderate:** *Facón Gash* (deep cut from gaucho knife fight), *Lobizón Bite* (carries a mild transformative curse), *Ghost Touch Frostbite* (localized cold damage from spectral contact).
- **Severe:** *Wekufe Infection* (a malevolent spirit has partially entered the body), *Full Moon Transformation* (uncontrolled Lobizón change), *Soledad Transparency* (body becoming partially invisible to the living).

**Social Conditions:**
- **Minor:** *Estancia Suspicion* (the ranch workers think you bring bad luck), *Gaucho Code Violation* (you have broken an unwritten rule of pampas hospitality).
- **Moderate:** *Hunted by the Rural Police* (the authorities are pursuing you for alleged sorcery), *Ghost Rider Debt* (you owe a favor to a spectral entity).
- **Severe:** *Mapuche Exile* (the Mapuche communities have declared you unwelcome), *Mandinga's Mark* (you made a bargain at the crossroads, and the payment is coming due).

**Psychological Conditions:**
- **Minor:** *Horizon Vertigo* (the infinite flatness of the pampas induces spatial disorientation), *Campfire Longing* (overwhelming desire for human company).
- **Moderate:** *Ghost Ear* (you hear the weeping of the Llorona at all times, even during the day), *Moonrise Dread* (escalating anxiety as the full moon approaches).
- **Severe:** *Pampas Dissolution* (you can no longer tell whether you are a living person who sees ghosts or a ghost who remembers being alive), *The Seventh Son's Hunger* (the Lobizón appetite has begun to leak into human consciousness).

## 7. Starter Scenario: The Estancia of Bones

**Framing:** A wealthy estancia owner has hired the Paisanos del Crepúsculo to investigate why his cattle are dying--found in the morning drained of blood with no visible wounds. His workers are terrified. Three have deserted. The estancia sits on former Mapuche sacred ground that was seized during the Conquest of the Desert, and the Machi suspects the deaths are connected to a wekufe that has been released from a desecrated burial site beneath the corral. But there is a complication: the estancia owner's seventh son has just turned thirteen, and tonight is a full-moon Friday.

**3-Layer Scene Design:**

1. **Immediate Threat:** The cattle deaths are caused by a *Wekufe Vampiro*--a malevolent spirit that has taken the form of a Luz Mala swarm infesting the corral. Each night, the swarm drains one animal. Tonight, with the boundary weakened by the desecrated burial ground, it will graduate to human prey.

2. **Ticking Clock:** Moonrise in 5 beats. The estancia owner's seventh son will begin his first Lobizón transformation. He does not know about the curse. His father does but has said nothing. When the boy transforms, his terror and confusion will draw every ghost rider within ten leagues to converge on the estancia.

3. **Opportunity:** The Machi can reseal the desecrated burial ground, banishing the wekufe, but the ritual requires the estancia owner's cooperation--specifically, his acknowledgment that the land was stolen. He must speak the truth of what happened to the Mapuche who lived here. This is not a magical requirement; it is a moral one. The land's spirit cannot heal while the crime is denied.

**Antagonist:** ***Don Estanislao Gutiérrez*** -- The estancia owner. He is not a villain but a product of his era--a man who inherited stolen land and built his identity on the fiction that it was always his. He loves his sons. He knows about the seventh-son curse. He has spent thirteen years dreading this night. He is also terrified that acknowledging the Mapuche claim to the land will cost him everything he has. He is a good man trapped by a lie he did not originate but has maintained.
- **Traits:** Wealthy, influential, genuinely loves his family, armed with political connections and a Winchester rifle, weakness to his sons' safety (he will do almost anything to protect them), strength of denial (he has convinced himself so thoroughly that the land is rightfully his that spiritual truth-reading comes back inconclusive).
- **Condition Track:** ☐ In Denial (refuses to discuss the land's history, offers money instead) / ☐ Confronted (presented with evidence of the desecrated burial, growing afraid) / ☐ The Son Transforms (witnessing his boy become a Lobizón shatters his worldview) / ☐ Truth Spoken or Refused (he either acknowledges the Mapuche claim, enabling the ritual, or he doubles down on denial, forcing the Paisanos to find another way).
- **Shadow Cost:** If Don Estanislao speaks the truth, the wekufe is banished and the Lobizón transformation is eased (not cured, but manageable). If he refuses, the wekufe must be defeated by force (much harder), and the Lobizón boy's first transformation will be violent and traumatic. Either way, the ghost riders are coming, and someone needs to ride out and meet them before they reach the estancia.

## 8. Why This Setting Matters

Argentina's gaucho tradition and its supernatural folklore represent one of the most psychologically profound ghost-story traditions in the world. The pampas are not merely haunted by individual spirits; they are haunted by *history*--by the unacknowledged violence of colonial displacement, by the ghosts of those who were erased from the land's story. The Soledad mechanic captures a truth about the human condition: we need each other not merely for companionship but for ontological stability. Without community, we become transparent. Without truth, the land itself sickens. And without a good asado with friends under the stars, even the bravest gaucho will lose themselves to the infinite grass.
