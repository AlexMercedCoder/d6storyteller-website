> - Father Callum Ashe, St. Maren's Cathedral

## 1. Setting Overview & Tone

St. Maren's Cathedral has stood at the center of the city for four hundred years. Its bells have tolled for funerals, festivals, and emergencies. They have never tolled without someone pulling the rope, until six weeks ago, when the bells rang a death knell at 2 AM for a man who died the following afternoon. Since then, the bells have rung fourteen times. Fourteen people have been identified by their patterns. Eleven have died. Three are still alive, and their clocks are ticking.

**Tone:** Somber, urgent, and laced with the specific dread of hearing your own name called before you are ready. The setting is a mystery wrapped in a theological question: are the bells predicting death or causing it? If they are predicting, can the prediction be changed? If they are causing, who is pulling the rope?

**Sensory Pillars:**
1. **Sight:** The cathedral at night: stone walls lit by streetlamps, the bell tower rising above the surrounding buildings, dark against a sky that the bells' vibrations seem to shake. The ropes in the bell chamber, hanging slack, motionless, while the bells above them swing in their frames. The faces of the people whose patterns have been identified: ordinary people living ordinary lives, carrying the knowledge that something ancient and accurate has announced their end.
2. **Sound:** The bells themselves: deep bronze tones that carry across the entire city, vibrating in the chest and the teeth. The death knell pattern: a specific sequence of tolls that the bell-ringers can decode, identifying the person by birth year and parish with a precision that predates electronic records. The silence after the bells stop, which is not relief but anticipation.
3. **Touch & Smell:** The cold stone of the cathedral interior, which holds the bells' vibration long after they have stopped. The rope in the bell chamber, rough hemp that is cold to the touch even when the bells are warm from ringing. The smell of the cathedral: incense, old stone, candle wax, and the metallic trace of bronze that hangs in the air after the bells have tolled.

## 2. The City

**St. Maren's Cathedral** is the center of the mystery. Its bell tower contains six bells, each one cast at a different foundry over a span of two centuries. The bells are maintained by a team of three volunteer ringers who have been ringing them for collective decades. None of the ringers can explain the autonomous tolling. The bell mechanism is mechanical, not electrical. There is no timer, no motor, and no remote activation. The bells ring because the bells decide to ring.

**The Rectory** is Father Callum Ashe's residence, adjacent to the cathedral. It has become the informal headquarters of the investigation: a place where the bell-ringers, the identified individuals, and the investigators meet to share information and plan responses. Father Ashe is a practical priest who treats the bells as a pastoral emergency rather than a miracle, which keeps the investigation grounded.

**The Identified Homes** are the residences of the three living people whose patterns the bells have tolled. Each person has been notified (by Father Ashe, personally, which he considers the worst part of his job). Each person has responded differently: one has barricaded himself in his house, one has continued her routine with deliberate normalcy, and one has come to the Rectory and asked what she needs to do differently.

**The Bell Foundry Records** are archived at the city's historical society. The bells were cast between 1623 and 1847. The foundry records include specifications, commissioning documents, and a letter from the 1623 founder that describes the largest bell as "cast with intention, to serve as both instrument and witness." The letter does not clarify what the bell was intended to witness.

## 3. Factions

**The Bell-Ringers** are three volunteers who have maintained St. Maren's bells for years. Head ringer Dora Marsh has been ringing for twenty years and knows every pattern in the traditional repertoire. She was the first to identify the autonomous tolling as death knells. She is frightened, competent, and refuses to leave the bell tower unmanned because she believes the bells are trying to communicate and someone should be listening.

**Father Ashe** and his small pastoral staff manage the human side of the crisis: notifying the identified, counseling the terrified, and fielding questions from a congregation that is split between awe and panic. Ashe does not believe the bells are divine. He believes they are a phenomenon that the cathedral's tradition has a responsibility to address. He is running out of answers and running low on sleep.

**The Identified** are the three living people whose death knells have tolled. Marcus Wren (the schoolteacher) has accepted the situation with a composure that worries his friends. Lena Soh (a city planner) has demanded an investigation and is funding it personally. Tom Radek (a retired firefighter) has locked his doors and is not answering calls. Each person's situation is different, but the pattern suggests their deaths are connected to choices they will make in the near future.

**The Historical Society** is represented by archivist Petra Voss, who has been researching the bells' history since the first autonomous tolling. Her findings suggest that the bells have rung autonomously before: three documented instances in the eighteenth century, each one associated with a cluster of deaths that the parish records describe as "prevented" without further explanation. The records imply that the bells were not predicting deaths but warning about them, and that previous generations knew how to respond.

## 4. Unique Mechanic: Toll Clock

When the bells ring for a specific person, a **Toll Clock** begins at 0 and counts toward 6.

**Clock advancement:** +1 per session (the death approaches naturally)

**Accelerating the clock (+1 additional):**
- The identified person makes the choice the bells are warning about
- An investigator's action inadvertently pushes the person toward the predicted outcome
- The person ignores a clear warning sign

**Slowing the clock (-1):**
- Identifying the specific choice or event the bells are warning about
- Helping the identified person understand their situation without forcing a decision
- Discovering and applying the response method used in previous centuries

**At clock 6:** The predicted death occurs unless the cause has been specifically addressed. The death is never arbitrary: it is always connected to a choice the person was going to make, a confrontation they were going to avoid, or a truth they were going to deny.

**At clock 0 (through intervention):** The bells ring a different pattern: not a death knell but something the ringers do not recognize. The identified person is safe. The choice they were warned about has been addressed.

## 5. Character Archetypes

### The Head Bell-Ringer

You have been ringing these bells for twenty years. You know their voice, their weight, and their resonance. You were the first person to hear them ring without a hand on the rope. You are terrified and determined: terrified because you do not understand what is happening, determined because you believe the bells are trying to help and someone needs to translate.

- **Drive:** *"Learn what the bells are saying well enough to help the people they are ringing for."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 3 | Mind 2 | Presence 2

### The Identified Planner

You are Lena Soh. The bells rang your pattern four days ago. You are a city planner, a pragmatist, and a person who does not accept prophecy as an explanation for anything. You are funding the investigation because you refuse to wait for a prediction to come true. You are also, for the first time in your career, examining your own upcoming decisions with the uncomfortable awareness that one of them might kill you.

- **Drive:** *"Find the mechanism behind the prediction and dismantle it before my clock runs out."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 1 | Mind 3 | Presence 3

### The Cathedral Archivist

You research the bells' history. Your archives contain records that previous generations left about the autonomous tolling, and those records suggest that the bells' warnings can be answered. The answer involves a ritual, a response, and a specific action by the identified person. You are piecing together the method from fragments. You are not certain it will work. You are certain that nobody else is looking.

- **Drive:** *"Reconstruct the old response before the current clocks run out."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 2 | Mind 4 | Presence 1

## 6. Starter Scenario & Why It Matters

**Three Clocks Running:** Marcus, Lena, and Tom each have a Toll Clock that is advancing. Marcus's is at 3 (his bell rang first). Lena's is at 2. Tom's is at 1. Father Ashe has discovered that all three were involved in a city council decision six months ago that rezoned a parcel of land containing a chapel associated with St. Maren's original parish. The chapel was demolished for a development project. Petra Voss has found an eighteenth-century account that describes the bells' autonomous tolling beginning after a similar demolition and stopping after the community "restored what was taken." Dora Marsh has noticed that the bells' pattern includes a note that is not part of any death knell: a note that the ringers call the "answer tone," used in traditional ringing to signal that a message has been received. Someone needs to ring the answer tone. Nobody alive knows the sequence. The characters must find the sequence, decode the bells' warning, and help three people confront the consequence of a decision they have already made.

**Why It Matters:** The Bell That Answers asks whether a warning from an inhuman source deserves a human response. The bells are not punishing the identified. They are alerting them. The deaths are not sentences. They are consequences of choices that the identified can still change. The setting is about accountability, community, and the specific difficulty of responding to a warning when you do not understand the language it is delivered in.
