> *"Yesterday, rent was illegal. Today, it is mandatory. Tomorrow, the building might not exist. I have lived here for thirty years and I vote every morning. I still cannot tell you whether this system is freedom or madness. It might be both."*
> - Mira Solvek, Revision District resident

--

## 1. Setting Overview & Tone

One morning, rent may be impossible. The next, every door may open for its owner. The vote is genuine, but campaigns have learned to weaponize ambiguity. Characters are organizers, clerks, reporters, and ordinary citizens trying to keep their community intact in a city where reality is subject to daily revision.

The city survives because the Mandate never lasts longer than one day. That limit creates a political culture built around urgency, loopholes, and exhaustion. Wealthy interests buy midnight canvassing. Neighborhoods organize dawn kitchens so people can vote before work. Every resident has a story about a measure that helped them and another that changed something they loved without asking.

**Tone:** Frantic, communal, and politically sharp. The setting takes democratic process seriously and then asks what happens when the process has the power to rewrite physics. The humor is the humor of civic life amplified: petty neighborhood disputes become reality-warping crises, and a zoning argument can literally move a building.

**Sensory Pillars:**
1. **Sight:** The dawn light hitting the counting engines on the roof of Dawn Hall, gears spinning as ballots are tallied. The physical shift of the city at sunrise: walls rearranging, streets widening or narrowing, a bridge appearing where yesterday there was a gap. The lingering afterimage of yesterday's reality, visible for about an hour as a faint shimmer in the air where things used to be.
2. **Sound:** The dawn bell that signals the vote is closed and the count is beginning. The grinding noise of architecture reshaping itself, like stone being carved at high speed. The shout of a Ward Kitchen organizer reading a measure aloud at 4 AM, translating legal language into plain speech for a crowd eating breakfast in the dark.
3. **Touch & Smell:** The specific vibration underfoot when the ground is rearranging. The smell of fresh mortar in a building that did not exist yesterday. The disorienting sensation of walking through a door that now leads somewhere different, which feels like a missed step on a staircase stretched across your entire sense of direction.

## 2. Civic Geography

**The Dawn Hall** contains the counting engines and the public gallery where results are announced. A measure must be read aloud before it becomes real, which gives skilled speakers one last chance to expose wording nobody noticed. The Hall Warden, a woman named Ilsa Chen, has held the position for fourteen years. She reads every measure in the same flat, neutral tone, refusing to inflect. She considers emphasis a form of influence.

**The Ward Kitchens** are mutual-aid spaces where organizers explain proposed measures in plain language. They serve food, distribute pamphlets, and run through the likely effects of each measure. They are also where people remember yesterday's reality long enough to compare it with today's. The kitchens are the most trusted institutions in Aurorath and the most targeted by Campaign Houses seeking early influence.

**The Revision District** is a neighborhood repeatedly transformed by close votes. Its residents have become experts in temporary architecture, legal memory, and moving quickly when a street changes direction. Buildings in the Revision District are designed to survive transformation: flexible foundations, modular walls, furniture that can be carried. The district's unofficial motto is "Build light."

**The Midnight Quarter** is where Campaign Houses operate. Between sundown and dawn, the Quarter hums with strategy sessions, canvassing dispatches, and the drafting of tomorrow's measures. The air smells like ink and coffee. The walls are covered with competing pamphlets. Access is technically open, but the Campaign Houses post their own security and decide who enters.

## 3. Factions

**The Referendum Office** keeps the voting process technically transparent. Its clerks count ballots, verify identities, and ensure the counting engines are calibrated. Head Clerk Tomás Serrano believes neutral procedure is their only protection from political intimidation. He is watched carefully by every faction because his procedural rulings determine which measures are eligible, and eligibility is power.

**The Campaign Houses** write proposals and sell turnout operations. They insist that persuasion is not coercion, even when a poorly worded measure makes a landlord's preferred interpretation physically true. The largest House, Compass North, controls roughly 30% of the city's canvassing infrastructure. Its director, Lena Voss, argues that professional campaigns are more accountable than amateur proposals. She is partially right and entirely self-serving.

**The Ward Assemblies** build local support networks and challenge measures that erase vulnerable communities. They disagree about whether to reform the system or use its own power to abolish it. Assembly Speaker Jiro Nakamura advocates for a constitutional amendment limiting what the Mandate can change. His proposal has been voted down six times. It keeps coming back.

**The Memory Keepers** are a small group that maintains records of what Aurorath looked like before each day's vote. They photograph buildings, map streets, and interview residents about what they remember. Their archive is the only continuous record of the city's history, because the city itself changes too fast for official records to keep up. They are not a political faction. They are a historical one, which in Aurorath amounts to the same thing.

## 4. Unique Mechanic: Civic Mandate

At the start of each session, write a proposed measure with the table. If it passes, establish one clear **Mandate** that changes the fiction until the next dawn.

Track two parallel clocks (3 segments each):

**Consensus** (public understanding and support):
- +1 when the characters explain the measure's intended effect in a public venue
- +1 when a Ward Kitchen successfully translates the measure into plain language
- +1 when evidence shows the measure working as intended

**Confusion** (misinterpretation and exploitation):
- +1 when an opponent exploits ambiguous wording
- +1 when the measure creates unintended hardship
- +1 when a Campaign House promotes a harmful interpretation

When Confusion exceeds Consensus, the Mandate manifests through the most harmful plausible reading. The players can reduce Confusion only through public action, not private cleverness. Democracy remains a shared task, not a puzzle to solve alone.

## 5. Character Archetypes

### The Ward Organizer

You can get a hundred people fed, informed, and moving before sunrise. You cannot make them agree. Your kitchen has been running for six years. You know every family in your ward, their schedules, their concerns, and which measures would help them. You also know that your ward represents about 4% of the total vote, which means organizing is necessary and insufficient.

- **Drive:** *"Keep my neighborhood real without treating every other ward as an enemy."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 2 | Mind 2 | Presence 3

### The Referendum Clerk

You know every procedural safeguard and which ones powerful people have learned to evade. You have worked the counting engines for nine years. You have never made an error. You have also noticed patterns in the measures that pass: certain phrasing appears across proposals from different Campaign Houses, as if someone is coordinating language to exploit the engines' interpretation algorithm.

- **Drive:** *"Make the vote mean consent, even if telling the truth costs me my office."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 2 | Mind 3 | Presence 2

### The Memory Keeper

You document what the city was before today's vote changed it. You carry a camera, a notebook, and a map that is wrong by the time you finish drawing it. You are not political. You are historical. You have also noticed that certain neighborhoods are erased more often than others, and the pattern correlates with which wards have the lowest voter turnout.

- **Drive:** *"Record what was real before the vote decides what is true. Someone has to remember."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 3 | Mind 2 | Presence 2

## 6. Starter Scenario & Why It Matters

**The Narrow Majority:** A measure passes by one vote and changes a living neighborhood into a public park. Its 400 residents remember their homes, but city records and maps now say the park has always been there. The Campaign House responsible insists this was not its intended meaning. The residents are standing in grass where their apartments used to be, holding keys to doors that no longer exist. The Memory Keepers have photographs. The Referendum Office has the ballot count. The characters have until the next dawn to preserve evidence, organize a response, build support for a restoration measure, and decide whether to fix this neighborhood or expose a flaw in the entire Mandate system. The next morning's vote will either restore the homes or confirm the park. The Campaign Houses are already drafting tomorrow's measure.

**Why It Matters:** The City That Votes at Dawn takes the promise of democracy literally and asks whether it survives the weight. The Mandate is power shared equally, which means it is power used imprecisely, politically, and sometimes cruelly. The setting does not argue against democratic governance. It argues that democracy requires work: civic education, mutual aid, institutional trust, and the willingness to show up every morning knowing that yesterday's victory can be undone by breakfast. The characters cannot fix the system. They can make it work for the people standing in front of them.
