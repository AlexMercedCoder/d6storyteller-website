> *"I have won forty-three bouts. I am the most celebrated fighter in Varro. My face is on merchandise. Children chant my name. I owe the Arena Authority nine thousand marks in training fees, equipment assessments, medical costs, and the public entertainment tax that increases every time I win. I am worth more fighting than free. My contract is technically available for purchase. The price increases with my fame."*
> - Kael Dunn, ranked first, Varro Grand Arena

## 1. Setting Overview & Tone

Varro is a prosperous city built on entertainment revenue. The Grand Arena is its economic engine: a massive amphitheater that hosts gladiatorial combat, athletic exhibitions, and public spectacles. The fighters are the city's primary cultural export. Their images appear on posters, their victories are reported in the press, and their contracts are traded between promoters like commodities. The system is legal, voluntary, and structured so that the most talented fighters are the least likely to leave.

**Tone:** Physically intense, politically sharp, and built on the frustration of people who are simultaneously celebrated and trapped. The setting is a sports drama about labor, exploitation, and the specific cruelty of a system that rewards excellence with increased obligation.

**Sensory Pillars:**
1. **Sight:** The Grand Arena: tiered stone seating, a sand floor that is raked between bouts, and the private boxes of promoters and city officials who watch from comfort while fighters bleed below. The fighters' barracks: clean, functional, and posted with schedules, debt statements, and the ranking board that determines who fights whom. The city's streets: posters of famous fighters, merchandise stalls, and the particular energy of a population that treats combat as their primary form of entertainment.
2. **Sound:** The roar of 20,000 spectators, which in Varro sounds like hunger. The clash of weapons on the arena floor, which is choreographed enough to be entertaining and genuine enough to be dangerous. The quieter sounds of the barracks: fighters taping hands, stretching, and discussing strategy, with the debt ledger posted on the wall behind them.
3. **Touch & Smell:** The grit of arena sand, which gets into everything and smells like copper and heat. The weight of arena equipment, which is heavier and more ornate than functional combat gear because the audience expects spectacle. The sting of medical treatment after a bout, which is competent (the Arena Authority invests in its fighters) and expensive (every bandage is itemized).

## 2. The City

**The Grand Arena** is Varro's largest structure: an amphitheater seating 20,000, equipped with sand floors, mechanical lifts for dramatic entrances, and a medical facility beneath the stands that is the best-equipped in the city because the fighters are investments.

**The Fighters' Barracks** house the contracted gladiators in clean, monitored quarters. Each fighter has a private room, access to training facilities, and a debt statement posted on their door. The statement is updated weekly. The barracks master, a retired fighter named Hesse, considers the postings motivational. The fighters consider them surveillance.

**The Promoters' Row** is the commercial district where the arena's business is conducted: contract negotiations, merchandising deals, and the secondary market where promoters buy and sell fighter contracts. The most powerful promoter, Lara Venn, controls the contracts of eight ranked fighters and considers them assets rather than people, a distinction she articulates clearly and without embarrassment.

**The Free Quarter** is the neighborhood where retired fighters and bought-out gladiators live. It is modest, quiet, and populated by people with visible scars and modest pensions. The Free Quarter's unofficial mayor, a former champion named Deka Solis, runs a support network for fighters who are close to buying their contracts and need financial strategy.

## 3. Factions

**The Arena Authority** is the city's regulatory body for gladiatorial combat. Director Marius Cole manages the revenue, sets the entertainment tax rate, and determines contract terms. Cole is a bureaucrat, not a sadist. He runs the arena the way a CFO runs a company: efficiently, profitably, and with a genuine belief that the system works for everyone. He is not wrong about the revenue. He is wrong about the fighters.

**The Ranked Fighters** are the top competitors whose fame drives arena attendance. Kael Dunn holds the number-one ranking and has won 43 bouts. He is the arena's most profitable asset and its most indebted fighter. He is also the most visible advocate for contract reform, which puts him in the position of fighting for the system that traps him while arguing against it between bouts.

**The Contract Reformers** are a coalition of fighters, retired gladiators, and a single sympathetic city councilwoman named Yael Park. The Reformers want three changes: a cap on the entertainment tax, transparent debt accounting, and a mandatory buy-out option after a fixed number of wins. The Arena Authority has blocked every proposal because the reforms would reduce revenue. Park is building public support. The Authority is building counter-arguments.

**The Fans' League** is an organized group of dedicated spectators who attend every match and whose collective enthusiasm drives arena culture. Fan leader Marco Pell genuinely loves the sport and genuinely does not think about the financial structure that makes it possible. He is not callous. He is a consumer enjoying a product without examining its supply chain. His League has enormous influence over which fighters become famous and therefore which fighters accumulate the most debt.

## 4. Unique Mechanic: Freedom Ledger

Each fighter tracks **Credits** and **Debts** on their Freedom Ledger.

**Earning Credits:**
- Win a bout: +3 credits
- Win with crowd favor (spectacular performance): +5 credits
- Negotiate a bonus with a promoter (Presence roll): +1 to +3 credits
- Complete a special exhibition or charity bout: +2 credits

**Accumulating Debts:**
- Training facility use (per session): +1 debt
- Equipment upgrade or repair: +1 to +3 debt
- Medical treatment after a bout: +1 to +4 debt (based on injury severity)
- Living expenses (per session): +1 debt
- Entertainment tax (assessed per victory): +1 to +3 debt (scales with ranking)

**Freedom threshold:** When Credits exceed Debts, the fighter can petition for contract release. The petition is reviewed by the Arena Authority, which may impose additional conditions.

**The trap:** Higher-ranked fighters earn more per bout but also incur higher training costs, better equipment, more expensive medical care, and a larger entertainment tax. The system is designed so that success and debt grow at the same rate.

## 5. Character Archetypes

### The Champion

You are the best fighter in Varro. You are also the most indebted. Your victories earn credits that your training, equipment, and medical costs consume. You fight because you are good at it, because the crowd loves you, and because the alternative is defaulting on a contract that would strip your ranking and your income. You want freedom. Freedom wants more money than you have.

- **Drive:** *"Win enough to buy my contract before my body breaks, and use my fame to change the system that made me famous and kept me fighting."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 3 | Finesse 2 | Mind 1 | Presence 2

### The Reformer Councilwoman

You are a city councilwoman advocating for contract reform. You have the data: fighter debt grows 15% faster than fighter income at every ranking. The system is designed to retain, not release. You are building a coalition of sympathetic officials, retired fighters, and a public that loves the sport and has never examined its economics. You need a champion fighter to endorse the reforms publicly. Kael Dunn is willing but afraid of retaliation.

- **Drive:** *"Pass the reforms before the Authority blocks the vote, and accept that the system will fight harder to survive than I can fight to change it."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 2 | Mind 3 | Presence 2

### The Retired Champion

You bought your contract seven years ago after fourteen years in the arena. You know the system's math because you lived it. You run a support network for fighters approaching buyout, providing financial strategy, debt negotiation, and the specific kind of encouragement that only someone who has been where they are can offer. You are free. Your body is not: your knees, your shoulder, and three fingers remind you daily of the price.

- **Drive:** *"Help the next generation get out faster and cheaper than I did, and make the Free Quarter bigger than the Barracks."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 2 | Mind 2 | Presence 3

## 6. Starter Scenario & Why It Matters

**The Fiftieth Bout:** Kael Dunn's fiftieth victory would make him the most winning fighter in Varro's history. The Arena Authority has scheduled the bout as a public holiday. Merchandise sales are projected to set records. Promoter Lara Venn has offered Dunn a bonus that would bring him within reach of his buy-out, contingent on his performance being "championship-caliber," which means more spectacle, more risk, and more potential medical debt. Councilwoman Park has scheduled a reform vote for the day after the bout, hoping to leverage public attention. Deka Solis has discovered that the Arena Authority has quietly raised Dunn's entertainment tax assessment, which means his fiftieth victory will add more debt than any previous win. The characters must navigate the bout, the politics, and the math, knowing that Dunn's most important fight is not in the arena but in the ledger.

**Why It Matters:** The Champion's Debt asks who profits from excellence when excellence is a commodity. The fighters are not slaves. They signed contracts voluntarily. The system is legal. The mathematics of the system ensure that the best fighters, the most entertaining, the most beloved, are the ones least likely to achieve freedom, because their value to the system exceeds their ability to pay their way out. The setting is about labor, spectacle, and the specific injustice of a structure that rewards talent with obligation.
