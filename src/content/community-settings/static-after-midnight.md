## 1. Setting Overview & Tone

WKLT broadcasts from a studio that occupies a floor that does not exist in the building's official plans. The elevator skips from 13 to 15. The stairwell has a door marked 14 that opens only between 11:45 PM and 4:15 AM. The studio behind the door is fully equipped, perpetually maintained, and has been broadcasting since at least 1983. Nobody knows who built it. The station's license is registered to a company that dissolved in 1991.

The hosts are people who found the door, walked through it, and discovered they could do the work. The callers are people, or things shaped like people, reaching out from places that the geography of the ordinary world does not include.

**Tone:** Intimate, creepy, and grounded in the specific loneliness of late-night radio. The horror is not aggressive. It is the slow realization that the callers are real, that the places they describe exist somewhere, and that the signal connecting the studio to those places is not a radio frequency but something older and less understood.

**Sensory Pillars:**
1. **Sight:** The studio at night: soundproofed walls, amber VU meters flickering with each word, the green glow of the phone board showing incoming calls. The city visible through the studio window: dark buildings, scattered lit windows, and the particular emptiness of streets at 2 AM. The static on the monitors during a difficult call, which sometimes forms shapes that look like the caller's environment.
2. **Sound:** The quality of the callers' voices: some crisp and immediate, others layered with background noise from places that carry their own acoustics (the echo of a hospital corridor, the creak of a ship's hull, the wind of a town that is not on any map). The hiss of dead air between calls, which is not silence but a low, shifting tone that the engineers have never been able to identify or eliminate.
3. **Touch & Smell:** The warmth of the studio equipment, which runs hotter than it should. The vibration of the phone receiver during an incoming call from an impossible location, which is different from a normal call: steadier, deeper, felt in the wrist. The smell of the studio: old carpet, electronics, coffee, and something faintly metallic that intensifies when the signal is strong.

## 2. The Station

**Studio A** is the primary broadcast space: a glass-walled room with two microphones, a phone board, and a broadcast console. The glass looks out on the city in one direction and the hallway in the other. During the strongest calls, the city view changes: the skyline shifts, buildings appear that were demolished decades ago, or the sky lightens with a dawn that is not happening outside the other windows.

**The Phone Board** is the station's most important and least understood piece of equipment. It has twelve incoming lines. Lines 1 through 9 receive ordinary calls from listeners. Lines 10 through 12 receive the impossible ones. The hosts cannot control which line rings. They can decline to answer, but the board records every unanswered call, and the recordings contain audio that was not transmitted through any detectable signal.

**The Archive Room** contains cassette recordings of every broadcast since 1983. The tapes are organized by date and cataloged by a volunteer archivist named June Halsey, who has been transcribing the impossible calls and cross-referencing them with historical records. Her findings are extensive, disturbing, and stored in a filing cabinet that she locks every morning when she leaves.

**The Hallway** connects the studio to the stairwell door. It is twenty feet long and should not exist: the building's floor plan does not include space for it. The hallway's lights are always on. The walls are painted institutional green. There are four doors other than the studio entrance, and all of them are locked. The hosts have tried to open them. The locks are warm.

## 3. Factions

**The WKLT Crew** runs the station: two rotating hosts, an engineer, and the archivist. Lead host Dex Morrow has been working the midnight shift for three years. He treats the impossible callers with the same professional courtesy he gives every caller, which is either admirable discipline or a coping mechanism. Engineer Kenji Sato maintains the equipment and has documented seventeen technical anomalies that he cannot explain. He is methodical, nervous, and recording everything.

**The Callers** are not a unified group. They are individuals reaching out from places that do not exist in normal geography: a burned town, a sunken ship, a sealed hospital ward, a highway that does not appear on any map. Some callers are coherent, frightened, and seeking help. Others are confused, repeating the same phrases across multiple broadcasts. A few are calm and knowledgeable, offering information that the hosts cannot verify but that later proves accurate. The most frequent caller, a woman who identifies herself as "Dr. Vance from the Oleander Ward," has been calling every Thursday for eleven months.

**The Building Management** is represented by a property manager named Alston Greaves, who officially denies that the fourteenth floor exists and privately leaves the stairwell door unlocked every night. Greaves inherited the building from his father, who inherited it from his father, and the family's relationship with the fourteenth floor is documented in a sealed letter that Greaves has not opened. He is not curious. He is afraid.

**The Signal Researchers** are a group of three academics who discovered WKLT's broadcast through frequency analysis and have been monitoring the station remotely. They believe the studio is a fixed point in a network of locations that exist between established geographies. Their lead researcher, Dr. Cora Lim, wants access to the studio. She has offered the crew funding in exchange for data. The crew is considering the offer because the station has no revenue and the equipment needs maintenance.

## 4. Unique Mechanic: Signal Clarity

Track a shared **Signal Clarity** score from 0 to 6, starting at 3.

**Raising Clarity (+1 each):**
- Take a call from an impossible location and engage genuinely (listen, respond, treat the caller as real)
- Follow up on a caller's information and verify it against historical records
- Maintain the studio equipment (engineering work that keeps the signal strong)
- Act on a caller's request without exploiting the information for personal gain

**Lowering Clarity (-1 each):**
- Ignore or dismiss an incoming call on lines 10-12
- Exploit a caller's information for profit, fame, or leverage
- Allow fear to cut a call short without proper sign-off
- Bring an outsider into the studio without the crew's consensus

**Thresholds:**
| Clarity | Signal Quality |
|---------|---------------|
| 5-6 | Crystal clear. Callers can describe their locations in detail. Background sounds are audible. The studio window shows the caller's environment. |
| 3-4 | Functional. Callers are audible but occasionally interrupted by static. Some details are lost. |
| 1-2 | Degraded. Calls are fragmentary. Voices overlap with unidentified transmissions. The hallway lights flicker. |
| 0 | Dead signal. No impossible calls come through. The locked doors in the hallway begin to rattle. Something is trying to use the signal from the other direction. |

## 5. Character Archetypes

### The Midnight Host

You sit behind the microphone from midnight to 4 AM and you talk to people who should not be able to call. You are good at this because you are good at listening, which is a rarer skill than speaking. You treat every caller with respect, whether they are calling from a suburb or a hospital that closed in 1987. Your therapist says you are using the show to avoid processing your own problems. Your therapist does not know about the fourteenth floor.

- **Drive:** *"Keep the signal clear long enough to help the callers, and figure out what the station actually is before someone less careful finds the door."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 2 | Mind 2 | Presence 3

### The Station Engineer

You maintain the equipment. You have documented seventeen anomalies in the broadcast signal, three instances of audio appearing on recordings that was not present during the live broadcast, and one case where the phone board received a call on a line that is not connected to anything. You are an empiricist in a situation that resists empiricism. You keep records because records are the only thing between you and the conclusion that none of this is real.

- **Drive:** *"Understand the signal well enough to control it, or at least well enough to shut it down if we need to."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 3 | Mind 3 | Presence 1

### The Signal Researcher

You found WKLT's broadcast through frequency analysis and you have been monitoring it from a university lab for six months. The signal does not behave like any known radio transmission. It occupies a frequency band that should not carry audio. You want access to the studio because your remote data is incomplete. You are also aware that the broadcast is the most significant anomalous phenomenon documented in the last century, and you are the only person with the training to study it properly.

- **Drive:** *"Get inside the studio and document the signal before the crew's goodwill runs out or the phenomenon stops."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 1 | Mind 4 | Presence 2

## 6. Starter Scenario & Why It Matters

**The Oleander Call:** Dr. Vance, the Thursday caller, has not called for two weeks. When she finally reaches the station, her voice is different: faster, frightened, and interrupted by sounds the crew has not heard before. She says the Oleander Ward is changing. The corridor outside her office is longer than it was last week. There are new rooms. The nurses without faces have begun opening doors they previously ignored. She gives the crew a specific set of instructions: a frequency, a time, and a phrase. She says that if the crew broadcasts the phrase on that frequency at that time, it will open a door in the hallway. She says the door leads to the Ward. She says she needs to be brought out. The crew must decide whether to trust a woman they have never met who is calling from a place that does not exist, and whether opening a door between the studio and the Ward is a rescue or an invitation.

**Why It Matters:** Static After Midnight asks what we owe the voices on the other end of the line. The callers are real. Their places are real. The signal connects a studio to locations that the ordinary world has lost, forgotten, or sealed. The characters are not ghostbusters or exorcists. They are radio professionals doing a show that happens to reach places nobody else can contact. The setting is about the intimacy of late-night broadcasting, the trust between a host and a caller, and the specific kind of courage it takes to pick up a phone when you know the person on the other end is calling from somewhere impossible.
