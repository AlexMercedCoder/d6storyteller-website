## 1. Setting Overview & Tone

The Haldane Collection occupies a converted townhouse in the arts district. Its twenty-three portraits are arranged across three floors, each one painted in a hyperrealistic style by artists whose names appear in no catalogue. The subjects are vivid, expressive, and incomplete: each painting is missing a specific quality that a complete human portrait would convey. The incompleteness is subtle. A casual visitor might notice that Portrait 12's eyes seem hollow, or that the subject of Portrait 3 appears to be listening for something. An extended visit reveals the truth: the portraits are finishing themselves, one visitor at a time.

**Tone:** Unsettling, intellectual, and focused on the specific horror of losing a piece of yourself so gradually that you do not notice until someone else does. The museum is quiet, tasteful, and predatory in the way that well-curated spaces can be: it makes you want to stay, and staying is the mechanism of harm.

**Sensory Pillars:**
1. **Sight:** The gallery: polished hardwood floors, carefully angled lighting, and the portraits themselves, which are arresting in a way that goes beyond artistic skill. The subjects look alive. Their skin has texture. Their clothing has weight. The spaces where they are incomplete are visible only if you know to look: a face with every feature except the lines around the eyes that indicate humor, hands that are perfectly rendered but posed without the casual ease that suggests warmth.
2. **Sound:** The quiet of the museum, which is the particular acoustics of a space designed for contemplation. The absence of sound from the portraits, which is notable because the paintings are so realistic that a viewer expects to hear breathing. The subtle change in a visitor's voice after extended exposure: a loss of resonance, a flatness, a quality that friends will notice before the visitor does.
3. **Touch & Smell:** The temperature in the gallery, which is cool and consistent, maintained for the paintings' preservation. The frames, which are warm to the touch regardless of the room temperature, as if the portraits generate their own heat. The smell of the museum: old wood, canvas, and a faint chemical trace that the curator attributes to preservative and that an expert would not recognize.

## 2. The Museum

**The First Floor Gallery** contains seven portraits, each missing a common social trait: humor, patience, warmth, tact, curiosity, generosity, and trust. These portraits are the most frequently visited and the most active. The first floor is where most thefts occur because visitors linger longest with portraits they find relatable.

**The Second Floor Gallery** contains nine portraits missing more complex traits: courage, creativity, empathy, conviction, resilience, nostalgia, ambition, tenderness, and spite. The second floor is less trafficked, which means its portraits are less complete and more aggressive in their acquisition. A visitor who reaches the second floor is typically already diminished from the first.

**The Curator's Office** is a private room on the third floor, accessible only to the curator. It contains seven additional portraits that are not displayed publicly. These portraits are nearly complete, missing only one trait each. The curator, Judith Haldane, keeps them private because a nearly complete portrait is an unstable one: it needs only one more visitor to finish, and a finished portrait does something that Judith has witnessed twice and does not want to see again.

**The Restoration Studio** is a converted attic space where Judith examines and maintains the portraits. The studio contains the tools of a conservator (brushes, solvents, magnifying equipment) and the tools of something else: journals from previous curators describing the portraits' behavior, a chart tracking which traits have been taken from which visitors, and a set of instructions, handwritten in 1894, describing how to "feed" a portrait without using a living visitor.

## 3. Factions

**Curator Judith Haldane** inherited the Collection from her mother, who inherited it from her grandmother. Judith is a genuine art conservator who considers the portraits her responsibility rather than her property. She understands that the portraits steal traits. She considers the theft a symptom of the portraits' incompleteness, not malice. She believes that completing a portrait (restoring all its missing traits) will release the subject and stop the theft. She has been working toward this for fifteen years. She has completed two portraits. The process is difficult, risky, and not entirely under her control.

**The Diminished** are former visitors who have lost traits to the portraits. Their organizer, a man named Callum Frost (who lost his sense of humor to Portrait 1 and has not laughed since), has been collecting testimonies and building a case against the museum. He wants the Collection shut down. He also wants his humor back, which requires cooperation from a portrait that is not interested in returning what it has taken.

**The Art Scholars** are academics who study the Collection for its artistic merit and have begun noticing its anomalous properties. Lead researcher Dr. Yael Stern has documented statistical changes in visitors' self-reported emotional assessments before and after museum visits. Her data shows a consistent pattern of trait reduction that she cannot explain through conventional psychology. She wants access to the curator's records. Judith is stalling.

**The Painted Subjects** are the people in the portraits. They are not dead. They are not alive. They are preserved in paint, incomplete, and aware. The most communicative subject, the woman in Portrait 7 (the patience thief), has been observed moving her hands in what Dr. Stern believes is sign language. The messages are fragmentary, but they suggest the subject knows she is incomplete and wants help finishing, not through theft, but through the method described in the 1894 instructions.

## 4. Unique Mechanic: Trait Theft

Each portrait is missing a specific trait. When a visitor spends time with a portrait:

**Roll Presence to resist:**

| Result | Outcome |
|--------|---------|
| **Best (6,6)** | No theft. The visitor's trait is too strong for the portrait to take. The visitor gains insight into the portrait's subject. |
| **Good (4-5)** | Partial resistance. The visitor feels a pull but retains their trait. They notice the portrait has changed slightly (more color, more detail). |
| **Adequate (2-3)** | Theft occurs. The visitor loses a measure of the corresponding trait. Related actions are at -1d6 until the trait is recovered. |
| **Failure (1)** | Significant theft. The visitor loses the trait noticeably. Friends and colleagues will observe the change. -1d6 on related actions, and the loss is visible to others. |

**Recovery:** A stolen trait can be recovered by:
- Confronting the portrait and understanding the subject's story (Mind roll)
- Completing the portrait through the 1894 method (requires the curator's instructions)
- Convincing the portrait to return the trait voluntarily (Presence roll, only possible if the portrait's subject is communicative)

**Completing a portrait:** When all missing traits are restored (not through theft but through the 1894 method), the portrait releases its subject. The release is dramatic: the painting goes blank, the stolen traits return to their original owners, and the subject appears briefly in the gallery before departing. Where they go is unknown.

## 5. Character Archetypes

### The Diminished Visitor

You visited the museum six months ago and stayed too long in front of Portrait 1. You have not laughed since. Your friends have noticed. Your relationships are suffering. You have tried therapy, medication, and distraction. Nothing works because the problem is not psychological. Your humor is hanging in a frame in a museum, and the painted face that stole it is smiling now in a way it was not smiling before your visit.

- **Drive:** *"Get my trait back from a painting, and make sure the museum cannot do this to anyone else."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 2 | Finesse 1 | Mind 2 | Presence 3

### The Reluctant Curator

You inherited a museum of predatory portraits and the responsibility of managing them. You are a conservator, not a jailer, but the distinction blurs when your charges steal from the people who come to see them. You believe the portraits can be completed and released. You have done it twice. Each time was dangerous. You are working on the third, and the third portrait is nearly complete, which makes it the most dangerous thing in the building.

- **Drive:** *"Complete enough portraits to prove the method works, before the Diminished or the scholars force me to shut down the only place that can help the subjects inside the frames."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 2 | Mind 3 | Presence 2

### The Art Researcher

You study the Collection because the paintings are extraordinary and because the data you have collected suggests they are doing something to visitors that conventional art theory cannot explain. You are a scientist in an art museum, and your evidence is compelling but inadmissible. You want access to the curator's records because the 1894 instructions are the key to understanding the mechanism, and understanding the mechanism is the first step to controlling it.

- **Drive:** *"Document the portraits' behavior rigorously enough to explain it, and decide whether the explanation should be published or buried."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 1 | Mind 4 | Presence 2

## 6. Starter Scenario & Why It Matters

**Portrait 19:** The nearly complete portrait in the curator's private gallery, Portrait 19, is missing only one trait: forgiveness. Judith has been searching for the 1894 method to restore forgiveness without taking it from a visitor. She has not succeeded. The portrait's subject, a young man painted in 1903, has become increasingly agitated: his painted hands grip the frame's inner edge, his eyes track movement in the room, and last night, Judith found him standing rather than seated. He is trying to leave the frame. If he does so incomplete, without forgiveness, the restoration journals suggest the release will be violent and the subject will carry his incompleteness into the world. Callum Frost is demanding the museum close immediately. Dr. Stern has noticed the portrait's movement and wants to observe. The characters must find a way to complete Portrait 19, manage the threat of an incomplete release, and decide what the Haldane Collection deserves: preservation, shutdown, or something that no one has attempted, freeing the subjects one at a time.

**Why It Matters:** The Museum of Unfinished People asks what completeness means and who pays for it. The portraits are not villains. They are incomplete people, preserved in paint, trying to become whole through the only mechanism available to them. The theft is real and the damage is real, but the subjects' need is also real. The setting is about the tension between protecting visitors and helping the painted: two legitimate goals that the museum's current operation makes impossible to pursue simultaneously. The characters must find a method that serves both, or choose which group to sacrifice.
