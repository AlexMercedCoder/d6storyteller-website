## 1. Setting Overview & Tone

The rail is a shared sacrament. Each carriage holds written prayers, emergency supplies, and passengers who cannot otherwise cross hostile borders. The Church owns the timetable, but the crews know the actual network. When a powerful city demands priority while floodwater cuts a rural line, a switch decision becomes theology.

The characters work the lines, negotiate with station towns, and uncover why a sainted locomotive has begun refusing its assigned route.

**Tone:** Working-class sacred. The setting treats labor as the foundation of everything that matters. The theology is real but practical: miracles happen on the rail, but they happen because someone laid the track, maintained the switches, and stayed up all night reading signals. The divine is present in the work, not above it.

**Sensory Pillars:**
1. **Sight:** Steam rising from a locomotive at a platform in pre-dawn darkness, the brass fittings glowing with a warmth that is not entirely thermal. Signal lamps along a mountain pass, each one a different color corresponding to the type of cargo: white for passengers, gold for prayers, red for emergency supplies. The switchyard at night, a web of glowing tracks converging on the junction tower.
2. **Sound:** The nine bells of the main junction, each with a distinct pitch corresponding to a rail line. The low rumble of a sacred locomotive approaching, which workers say they can feel in their teeth before they hear it. The work songs of the Yardworkers' Communion, which encode routing information, weather warnings, and political news into lyrics that sound devotional.
3. **Touch & Smell:** The heat of a locomotive's boiler through iron plating. The smell of coal smoke, which clings to everything a railworker owns. The specific weight of a sealed prayer bag handed from platform to carriage: heavier than paper should be, though nobody can explain why. The cold mountain air at a border platform, where the wind carries the smell of different cooking from whichever side last held the territory.

## 2. The Prayer Rail

**The Switchyard of Nine Bells** is the largest junction in the nation. Each bell belongs to a different line, and a bell rings only when its train carries cargo that must not be delayed. Yard workers can hear the difference between a funeral bell and a famine bell, even when the Church insists all cargo is equal. Yard Chief Darke has managed the junction for twenty years and knows every sound the bells make, including the ones they are not supposed to make.

**The Reliquary Cars** carry letters, offerings, and sealed requests between shrines. Some prayers are private. Others are civic petitions that gather force as they travel. A train that arrives late can change a court ruling, a harvest blessing, or a ceasefire. The cars are maintained by dedicated custodians who ride with the cargo and are forbidden from reading it.

**The Border Platforms** are neutral ground by old treaty. In practice, soldiers search passengers, merchants charge desperate people double, and stationmasters maintain peace by knowing which rules must be followed exactly and which can be bent. Each border platform has a small chapel where travelers can add their prayers to the next outgoing train, regardless of which side of the border they stand on.

**The Ghost Lines** are abandoned routes that the Church has officially decommissioned. Some of them still carry trains. Yard workers report signals on dead lines, lanterns where no signal box exists, and the distant sound of wheels on track that was pulled up years ago. The Church calls these echoes. The workers call them debts: prayers that were never delivered, still looking for their destination.

## 3. Factions

**The Office of Sacred Timetables** controls official routes and presents delay as a moral failure. Its chief clerk, Sister Merel, believes central authority keeps the rail from becoming a set of private fiefdoms. She is under pressure to favor wealthy cities that subsidize the Church, and she is aware that her timetables increasingly serve politics rather than prayer. She has not yet decided what to do about it.

**The Yardworkers' Communion** maintains the rail and shares information through work songs encoded with routing data, weather patterns, and political intelligence. The Communion is preparing for a strike, but its members know that a total shutdown would abandon isolated towns that depend on the rail for medicine and food. Shop steward Kael Brenn is drafting a partial action: stop luxury service to wealthy cities while maintaining lifeline routes. The plan is controversial because it asks workers to choose whose suffering matters more.

**The Platform Compact** is a loose alliance of station towns that trades food, shelter, and local knowledge for Route Favor. Its leaders are tired of being described as essential whenever a city needs something and optional the rest of the year. Compact speaker Halene Voss is building a coalition to demand permanent representation on the Timetable committee, which the Church has blocked three times.

**The Locomotive Saints** are the sacred engines themselves. The oldest locomotives on the network have been in continuous operation for centuries, and the Church recognizes them as consecrated objects. Some workers believe the locomotives are more than objects: that centuries of carrying prayers have given them a form of intention. The evidence is circumstantial but persistent. Locomotives sometimes refuse routes, alter speed without engineer input, and ring their own bells when no one is in the cab.

## 4. Unique Mechanic: Route Favor

Each named rail line begins with **Route Favor** between 1 and 3.

**Gaining Favor (+1 each):**
- Deliver vital cargo safely despite hardship
- Fulfill a promise made to a station community
- Repair a route at personal cost
- Allow a community to set its own routing priority

**Spending Favor:**
- 1 Favor: Request a timely stop, find a trusted rail contact, or have a station provide modest aid
- 2 Favor: Reroute a train against normal procedure or ask a sainted locomotive for a miracle

**Debt:** When the characters use a route for private advantage, mark Debt on that line. At 3 Debt, the next station demands repayment before offering help. Repayment must be a concrete service, not a donation. The rail runs on relationships, not currency.

## 5. Character Archetypes

### The Signal Tender

You can read a line's mood from its lamps and have learned that the new red signal means fear, not danger. You maintain the signal boxes along Line Four, which runs through contested territory. You see every train that passes and know from the signals whether it carries prayers, passengers, or soldiers pretending to be passengers.

- **Drive:** *"Keep the rail public, even if that means telling my own union a difficult truth."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 3 | Mind 2 | Presence 2

### The Pilgrim Courier

You carry messages that cannot be trusted to any office. One of them is addressed to a locomotive. You travel the network on passenger passes, moving between stations with sealed letters, verbal messages, and occasionally people who need to cross borders without documentation. The Church considers you a nuisance. The Platform Compact considers you essential.

- **Drive:** *"Deliver the letter before the people who wrote it decide the answer would be too costly."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 2 | Finesse 2 | Mind 1 | Presence 3

### The Retired Engineer

You drove a sainted locomotive for thirty years before your hands became too unsteady for the controls. You know every mile of Line Two, including the Ghost Line section that the Church says does not exist. You have heard the bells ring on their own. You have felt the locomotive change speed without your input. You do not think the engines are alive. You think they remember.

- **Drive:** *"Pass on what I know about the locomotives before the Church rewrites what they are."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 2 | Finesse 1 | Mind 3 | Presence 2

## 6. Starter Scenario & Why It Matters

**The Unscheduled Stop:** A prayer train halts at an abandoned platform and will not move. Its cargo contains pleas from two cities about to make war. The Office orders the cars decoupled and the train pushed aside, but the locomotive has locked its own brakes. Yardworkers say that forcing the train would break a consecration older than the treaty. The platform, which was decommissioned eight years ago, sits above a collapsed tunnel where a previous train was buried after a rockslide. The characters must learn what the locomotive heard beneath the platform, negotiate with soldiers arriving from both directions, and decide which prayers deserve to reach their destination first.

**Why It Matters:** Saints of the Switchyard asks who decides what moves through a shared system and at what cost. The rail is infrastructure. It is also a covenant: the promise that even people who hate each other will not starve each other. The characters are workers, not rulers. Their power comes from the fact that without them, nothing moves. The setting asks whether that power is a privilege, a burden, or a sacred obligation, and whether the institution that claims to speak for the divine deserves to control the tracks.
