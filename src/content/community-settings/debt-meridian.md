## 1. Setting Overview & Tone

Meridix is a city built on extracted experience. The technology is clean, painless, and voluntary in the narrow legal sense: nobody is forced to sell memories, but when the alternative is homelessness, voluntary becomes a flexible word. The memory banks operate like financial institutions, purchasing experiences at market rate and encoding them for resale. The wealthy accumulate pleasant memories from strangers, living curated emotional lives. The poor sell joy, wonder, and love because those emotions are worth enough to cover necessities.

**Tone:** Noir, melancholic, and sharply observed. The setting is a crime story and an economic critique wrapped in cyberpunk aesthetics. The sadness is specific: a person who sold their mother's voice to pay a medical bill, a veteran who sold their combat memories and now cannot explain their flinch response, a musician who sold the afternoon they fell in love with music and cannot understand why they still play.

**Sensory Pillars:**
1. **Sight:** Meridix at night: neon signs advertising memory banks ("Sell your best for our best rate"), extraction clinics with clean white interiors, and the gray residential districts where people who have sold too many memories live in apartments they cannot remember choosing. The ledger display on every citizen's wrist implant, showing their memory balance: retained, sold, and outstanding debt.
2. **Sound:** The hum of an extraction chair, which is the quietest medical device in the city because the process involves listening to a memory as it leaves. The digital chime of a credit transfer. The silence in a memory support group, where people describe experiences they can no longer feel. The sound of a stolen memory alert: a wrist implant alarm that means someone has accessed your ledger without authorization.
3. **Touch & Smell:** The cold gel applied to temples before extraction, which smells like antiseptic and mint. The weight of a loaded memory chip, which is physically light but psychologically heavy. The disorientation of waking after an extraction, reaching for an experience that is no longer there: a phantom limb in the mind, a gap where continuity used to be.

## 2. The City

**The Memory Exchange** is Meridix's central market for memory currency. Licensed banks buy, sell, and trade encoded experiences. The Exchange's public floor is a chaotic, neon-lit trading floor where brokers shout prices for categories of memory: "childhood joy, grade A, 200 credits," "first snowfall, unverified, 40 credits." The back offices handle institutional clients: corporations buying employee training memories, governments purchasing intelligence, and collectors who accumulate specific emotional states.

**The Extraction Clinics** are scattered across the city. Licensed clinics are clean, professional, and regulated. Unlicensed clinics are cheaper, faster, and occasionally destructive: a poorly calibrated extraction can damage adjacent memories, leaving gaps in experiences the client did not intend to sell.

**The Hollow Districts** are neighborhoods where most residents have sold enough memories to affect daily function. Streets are quiet. Neighbors do not remember each other's names. Buildings are maintained by people who cannot remember moving in. Social services are minimal because the residents have sold the memories that would let them articulate what they have lost.

**The Black Ledger** is the criminal memory economy. Stolen memories are extracted through illegal devices, sold through underground brokers, and installed in buyers who want specific experiences without paying market rates. The Black Ledger's most profitable product is "identity kits": complete memory sets that allow a person to assume someone else's experiential history.

## 3. Factions

**Meridix Banking Consortium** controls the licensed memory market. Its CEO, Alexa Strand, is a careful executive who considers memory currency the most efficient financial system ever devised. She is aware of the system's inequities and considers them a regulation problem, not a design flaw. The Consortium's compliance department employs more people than its ethics board, which is a coincidence Strand has not addressed.

**The Memory Workers' Union** represents extraction technicians and clinic staff. Their members perform the extractions and see the human cost firsthand. Union president Dev Corro has been lobbying for mandatory cooling-off periods between extractions and a cap on the percentage of a person's memories that can be sold. The Consortium has blocked both proposals.

**The Recollection Network** is an underground organization that helps memory theft victims recover their stolen experiences. Their methods include tracking stolen memories through the black market, confronting buyers, and, when necessary, stealing memories back. Their leader, a woman who goes by Echo (she sold her real name), operates out of the Hollow Districts and considers herself a paramedic for the soul.

**The Archivists** are a philosophical movement that argues memories should not be treated as property. They maintain free memory archives where people can deposit copies of their experiences for safekeeping before selling the originals. The archives cannot restore a sold memory, but they can provide a recording: a third-person account of an experience the owner no longer has. The Archivists' founder, Dr. Yael Soren, considers the archives a form of protest. The Consortium considers them a market disruption.

## 4. Unique Mechanic: Memory Ledger

Each character maintains a **Memory Ledger** divided into three categories:

**Retained:** Memories the character still possesses. These function normally and provide the character's identity, skills, and relationships.

**Sold:** Memories the character has voluntarily exchanged for credits. Sold memories are gone. The character knows the memory existed (the ledger records the transaction) but cannot access the experience. A sold childhood memory means the character knows they had a childhood but cannot remember specific events.

**Stolen:** Memories taken without consent. Stolen memories create gaps that the character may not immediately notice. A stolen relationship memory means the character does not recognize someone they should know. A stolen skill memory means a competence they relied on is suddenly absent.

**Mechanical effects:**
- **Selling a memory:** Gain credits equal to the memory's emotional intensity (1-3). Lose access to related knowledge, skills, or relationships. If the sold memory is relevant to a future action, the character rolls at -1d6.
- **Memory theft:** The Storyteller removes a memory from the character's Retained list without announcement. The gap becomes apparent when the character attempts to use the missing experience.
- **Recovery:** Investigate the theft, track the stolen memory, and recover it. The recovered version may not perfectly match the original (memories degrade in transit), introducing minor discrepancies that the character must live with.

## 5. Character Archetypes

### The Extraction Tech

You operate the chair. You perform 15 extractions per day. You have watched people sell their weddings, their parents' faces, and their first moment of creative joy. You are good at the job. You are starting to question whether being good at this job is something to be proud of. You have not sold any of your own memories, which makes you unusual in your income bracket. You cannot afford not to. You are running out of alternatives.

- **Drive:** *"Find a way to pay rent without selling the parts of myself that make me worth knowing."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 3 | Mind 2 | Presence 2

### The Memory Detective

You work for the Recollection Network, tracking stolen memories through the black market. Your job is part investigation, part therapy: you find what was taken and help people reintegrate experiences that have been separated from them, sometimes for years. You carry a portfolio of stolen identities, each one a person who is missing part of themselves. You are motivated by the work. You are haunted by the cases you cannot solve.

- **Drive:** *"Return what was stolen before the gap becomes permanent and the person forgets they were ever whole."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 2 | Mind 3 | Presence 2

### The Hollow Resident

You live in the Hollow Districts. You have sold more memories than you can track. Your ledger shows 47 sold transactions. You know you had a family because your apartment has children's drawings on the refrigerator. You know you had a career because your closet contains a uniform you do not recognize. You are piecing yourself together from external evidence, and the person you are building does not match the person you were. You are not sure which one you prefer.

- **Drive:** *"Decide whether I want my old self back or whether the person I am building from the gaps is someone worth keeping."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 2 | Finesse 2 | Mind 2 | Presence 2

## 6. Starter Scenario & Why It Matters

**The Duplicate:** A woman walks into the Recollection Network's office and reports that someone is living her life. Not metaphorically. A buyer on the black market purchased a complete identity kit made from her stolen memories, and that buyer is now using her name, her career, her relationships, and her home. The original woman has been evicted from her own apartment by a person who remembers living there and she does not. The police consider it a civil dispute. The Memory Exchange says the transaction is outside their jurisdiction. The buyer, who genuinely believes the memories are theirs, is terrified of losing the life they have built. The characters must find the stolen memories, determine whether they can be returned without destroying the buyer's constructed identity, and confront the Black Ledger operation that made the theft possible.

**Why It Matters:** The Debt Meridian asks what you are when your experiences can be removed, sold, and installed in someone else. The memory economy is not a metaphor. It is the logical endpoint of treating every aspect of human experience as a commodity. The characters live in a city where the poorest people are literally selling who they are to survive, and the system calls this voluntary. The setting does not argue that memory technology is evil. It argues that a society that makes people choose between identity and survival has failed, regardless of how clean the technology is.
