> *"My mother died in March. In April, her kitchen grew a second window that looks out on a garden I have never seen. The garden has her roses in it. They are blooming. They are not real. They will disappear when I stop missing her, which means they will be here for a very long time."*
> - Verlaine resident, grief cartographer's field interview

--

## 1. Setting Overview & Tone

Verlaine's geography is shaped by loss. Every death creates a small anomaly: a place transformed by the mourning of the people who loved the dead. These places are personal, specific, and temporary. A fisherman's death might extend a harbor pier into a cove that only exists in the memory of a childhood fishing trip. A teacher's death might add a classroom to a school, fully furnished, containing the lesson she never finished. A soldier's death might create a road that leads home from a battlefield that no longer exists.

The Cartographers' job is to find these places, map them, and decide what to do with what they contain. Some grief-places hold nothing but atmosphere. Others hold objects of genuine value: letters, tools, heirlooms that the dead could not carry but that the mourning somehow preserved. A few hold messages, which the Cartographers treat with the reverence and caution of people opening mail addressed to someone who is grieving.

**Tone:** Tender, quiet, and respectful of sorrow. The setting is not dark. It is sad in the way that real grief is sad: specific, surprising, and interleaved with ordinary life. The Cartographers are professional, compassionate people who walk into other people's worst moments and try to be helpful without being intrusive. The political pressure to exploit grief-places is the setting's antagonist, not grief itself.

**Sensory Pillars:**
1. **Sight:** A grief-place appearing: a door in a wall where there was none, slightly ajar, opening onto a room that is lit from within by a light source that does not exist in the building's architecture. The geography inside: perfectly rendered environments from the dead person's memory, accurate to details the mourners recognize. The cartographer's map, drawn in ink that fades as the place dissolves.
2. **Sound:** The silence inside a grief-place, which is not absence but insulation, the sounds of the outside world muffled as if the place is holding its breath. The particular sounds the dead person loved, present inside: birdsong from a specific garden, the creak of a particular chair, a voice humming a melody the family recognizes. The sound of the place closing: a door settling shut, a path narrowing, geography contracting like a sigh.
3. **Touch & Smell:** The warmth of a grief-place, which is always comfortable. Objects inside can be touched, held, and used. They feel real because they were real to the person who died. The smell of the place matches the memory: bread baking, fresh-cut grass, a perfume the dead person wore. The sensation of crossing the threshold, which feels like stepping from one season into another.

## 2. The Landscape of Loss

**The Cartographers' Hall** is the guild headquarters: a building in the capital that is itself partially mapped from grief-places. Several rooms were originally anomalies from the deaths of former Cartographers, which their colleagues preserved by continuing to mourn. The building's architecture is impractical and beautiful, containing doors that open onto gardens that should not fit inside the structure and hallways that lead to rooms from three different eras.

**The Vigil Houses** are places where families can mourn in proximity to grief-places. The Houses provide shelter, food, and the specific privacy that grief requires. They are staffed by caretakers trained to recognize when a mourning person needs company and when they need to be left alone.

**The Crown Registry** is the government's attempt to catalogue grief-places for political and economic purposes. The Registry's assessors visit anomalies, determine their contents, and classify them by value. A grief-place containing a dead general's strategic maps is classified differently from one containing a grandmother's recipe collection. The Cartographers consider the classification system a violation. The Crown considers it efficient resource management.

**The Fading Margins** are areas where grief-places have dissolved so frequently that the geography is unstable. Walls are thin. Doors appear and disappear without a death to justify them. The Margins are disorienting, structurally unsound, and full of fragments: a doorknob from a house that disappeared six months ago, a flower growing in a crack that smells like a person nobody alive remembers.

## 3. Factions

**The Cartographers' Guild** maps grief-places and advocates for their respectful treatment. Guildmaster Elise Dann is a patient, principled woman who has been mapping anomalies for twenty-five years. She believes that grief-places belong to the bereaved and that the Cartographers' role is documentation, not extraction. She is losing the political argument to the Crown Registry because the Crown has a budget and the Guild has principles.

**The Crown Registry** classifies grief-places by military, economic, and intelligence value. Its director, Lord Ashworth, considers the anomalies a natural resource. He is not cruel. He is pragmatic in a way that treats mourning as an inconvenient delay between a death and a useful discovery. His assessors are trained to work quickly, which the Cartographers consider a form of disrespect.

**The Vigil Keepers** are caretakers and counselors who staff the Vigil Houses. They are the closest thing Verlaine has to grief therapists, and they have noticed something the other factions have not: the grief-places are getting larger. Recent deaths are producing anomalies that are more complex, more persistent, and harder to dissolve. The Keepers' senior counselor, Dr. Maron Falk, believes the kingdom's collective grief is accumulating rather than resolving, and the geography is responding.

**The Memory Sellers** are an underground market for objects extracted from grief-places. Some sellers are opportunistic. Others are bereaved families selling objects from their own grief-places because the Crown's tax assessments on anomalies have made mourning financially unsustainable. The market is technically illegal. It is also the only way some families can afford to keep their homes after a death.

## 4. Unique Mechanic: Mourning Clock

Each grief-place has a **Mourning Clock** with 6 segments. The clock counts down as the bereaved process their loss.

**Slowing the clock (-1 segment, minimum 0):**
- Visit the grief-place and honor the dead (tell stories, bring offerings, complete unfinished tasks)
- Resolve a conflict the dead person left behind
- Allow the bereaved to mourn at their own pace without external pressure

**Accelerating the clock (+1 segment):**
- Extract objects or information for non-memorial purposes
- Force the bereaved toward closure (tell them to move on, sell the house)
- Disturb the grief-place's contents without respect
- Crown Registry assessment (the bureaucratic process itself accelerates dissolution)

**When the clock fills:** The grief-place dissolves. Geography returns to normal. Any objects inside disappear. Any unresolved business remains unresolved. The bereaved feel the dissolution as a physical sensation: a mild shock, like the feeling of forgetting something important.

**Preservation:** A grief-place can be preserved indefinitely if the mourning is maintained. This is rare, expensive, and emotionally costly. The Cartographers' Hall contains preserved grief-places maintained by Guild members who have chosen to carry their mourning as a professional commitment.

## 5. Character Archetypes

### The Field Cartographer

You map grief-places. You knock on doors, introduce yourself to mourning families, and ask permission to document the anomaly before it disappears. You have mapped 300 places. Each one was specific, beautiful, and temporary. You carry a portfolio of maps to places that no longer exist, and sometimes families contact you years later, asking if you remember what their mother's garden looked like. You always do.

- **Drive:** *"Map every place I can reach, preserve what the bereaved want preserved, and resist anyone who treats grief as a resource to mine."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 3 | Mind 2 | Presence 2

### The Vigil Keeper

You sit with people who are mourning. You bring tea, adjust blankets, and know when to talk and when to stay silent. You have noticed that the grief-places are getting larger, and you are worried that the kingdom is accumulating sorrow faster than people can process it. You are not a politician. You are a caretaker. But someone needs to tell the Crown that grief is not a problem to be solved on a schedule.

- **Drive:** *"Protect the bereaved from a system that measures mourning in days and grief-places in assessed value."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 2 | Mind 2 | Presence 3

### The Crown Assessor

You classify grief-places for the Registry. You are efficient, methodical, and increasingly uncomfortable. Your last assessment covered a dead child's playroom. It contained toys, drawings, and a music box that played a lullaby the parents recognized. You classified it as "low value, no strategic content." You have been unable to sleep since because you know the classification will accelerate the place's dissolution, and the parents wanted more time.

- **Drive:** *"Find a way to do my job without destroying what I am cataloguing."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 2 | Mind 3 | Presence 2

## 6. Starter Scenario & Why It Matters

**The General's Map Room:** A retired general has died, and her grief-place has manifested as a war room containing detailed maps of a border region where the kingdom is currently negotiating a peace treaty. The maps include troop positions, supply lines, and strategic assessments that the Crown does not have. Lord Ashworth wants the maps extracted before the grief-place dissolves. Guildmaster Dann wants the family consulted first. The general's daughter, who is the primary mourner, has entered the grief-place and found something the Crown does not know about: a letter addressed to the enemy commander, proposing a unilateral ceasefire that the general was drafting when she died. The letter would undermine the Crown's negotiating position. The daughter wants to send it. The Crown wants it destroyed. The characters must decide what the general's final act deserves: completion, classification, or compassion.

**Why It Matters:** The Cartography of Grief asks how a society should treat the spaces that loss creates. The grief-places are not resources. They are not obstacles. They are the visible evidence of love, preserved in geography for as long as the love persists. The setting is about the tension between a society that values efficiency and the reality that grief is not efficient, not productive, and not something that should be measured, accelerated, or exploited. The characters walk into places made of mourning and must decide whether to map them, protect them, or let them go.
