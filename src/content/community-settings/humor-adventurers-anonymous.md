## 1. Setting Overview & Tone

**High-Concept Pitch:** The world has been saved repeatedly. The heroes who saved it need help.

**Adventurers Anonymous** exists because the mental health community eventually recognized that the compulsive-heroism pattern was real, diagnosable, and treatable. The adventuring reflex that saved civilizations was also wrecking relationships, causing property damage, and preventing former heroes from living functional lives in times of relative peace.

The players are part of this community: in recovery (genuinely trying to change), running the program (genuinely trying to help), or somewhere in the complicated middle where the right answer is unclear.

The comedy emerges from the genuine sincerity of the recovery effort colliding with a world that keeps having actual problems requiring heroic responses. Is it relapse if the dragon was real? The therapist says yes, but also notes that "the dragon was real" is something every member says.

**Tone:** This takes the support group format seriously while playing the adventure compulsion for comedy. The characters are genuinely trying. The world is not cooperating. Both are true.

**Sensory Pillars:**
1. **Sight:** A support group meeting room that is subtly, ominously like a dungeon antechamber (the building is old; they're working on moving), a circle of chairs occupied by beings who collectively could level a city but are currently focusing on emotional regulation, weapons stacked neatly by the door (there's a policy; members complied; some members are visibly twitchy about it), and a whiteboard with the twelve steps, several of which have been modified by committee after heated debate.
2. **Sound:** The specific cadence of a support group: the opening, the check-ins, the shares, the responses, the closing -- all practiced, genuine, occasionally derailed by someone noticing something concerning through the window, and the very specific sound of nine heroes simultaneously resisting the urge to investigate.
3. **Touch & Smell:** The institutional comfort of a community space trying to be welcoming, the faint metallic smell that adventurers carry (dungeon residue; it gets into everything), the worn texture of the meeting chair of a long-term member, and the warmth of a group that has genuinely helped each other even if they're also genuinely struggling.

**The Core Tension:** Recovery is real and valuable. The adventures are also real. Choosing which real thing to prioritize is the ongoing challenge.

## 2. AA Structure

### The Twelve Steps (Modified)
The AA program uses a twelve-step framework adapted for adventure addiction:
1. Acknowledge that questing has become unmanageable
2. Believe that recovery is possible without abandoning heroism entirely
3. Choose to recover on purpose, not just when the world is quiet
4-12. Progressive work on triggers, relationships, compulsions, and the definition of a genuine crisis vs. a situation that would be fine without intervention

### The Meeting
Weekly. Open format. Shares are voluntary. The group has established rules about weapons (stacked by door), active spells (disclosed at check-in), and prophecies (mentioned last; they derail everything if mentioned first).

### The Therapists
Trained professionals who mostly work with mundane clients and got into adventurer therapy by accident. They are very good at their jobs. They are also mildly terrified of their clients, which they manage professionally.

### The Genuine Crises
The world does not stop having crises because the heroes are in recovery. Real threats emerge. Real people need help. The group has developed a decision framework: before responding heroically, the member must complete a "Is This Mine to Do?" checklist. The checklist is contested. It exists.

## 3. Adventure Seeds

1. **The Dragon Was Real:** A member relapsed: entered a dungeon, cleared it, solved a regional crisis. They're back in the meeting, step count reset. Investigation reveals the dungeon was real and the crisis was genuine. But so was the compulsive choice to handle it alone without calling for support. How do we process both?

2. **The Targeted Meeting:** Someone is using the AA meeting location to stage quests, knowing the group will eventually respond to the escalating situations. Identifying who is doing this and why requires investigation -- which is itself a quest. The irony is noted in the meeting.

3. **The New Member:** A powerful, recently-retired hero joins the group. They are dismissive of the recovery framework. They are also clearly struggling. Working with them requires the group to function effectively despite a member who doesn't yet believe in the program.

4. **The Group Quest:** The group has collectively agreed to take on one legitimate crisis together, with full awareness and consent, as a therapeutic exercise in "chosen heroism vs. compulsive heroism." The quest is manageable. The group dynamics during it are not.

5. **The World Needs You:** An actual civilization-level crisis is emerging. Every member of AA has received the prophecy, the message, or the dream. They can see it coming. They have worked very hard on their recovery. This is a genuine situation. The group meeting to decide what to do is the most important meeting they've ever had.
