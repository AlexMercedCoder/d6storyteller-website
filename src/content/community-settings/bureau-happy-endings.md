## 1. Setting Overview and Tone

The Bureau of Happy Endings operates under the Ministry of Narrative Welfare and employs approximately two thousand agents in twelve regional offices. Bureau jurisdiction is specific: they do not improve lives, they improve life endings. An agent is not permitted to intervene in a client's ongoing circumstances beyond the scope of their assigned case narrative. An agent who falls in love with a client, for example, creates a conflict of interest that requires case reassignment and, in documented instances, its own Bureau intervention.

**Tone:** Warm, dry, and full of institutional absurdity. The comedy comes from the sincere application of bureaucratic procedure to questions that resist procedural answers. The Bureau genuinely wants to help. The Bureau's paperwork requirements make helping extraordinarily complicated.

**Sensory Pillars:**
1. **Sight:** The Bureau's regional office: an open-plan workspace with agents at their desks reviewing glowing life story files on monitors that display actual narrative threads in color-coded status indicators. The coffee machine that has been broken for three years and which nobody has reported because the form required is Form 17-C (Non-Essential Appliance Failure) and nobody knows where the 17-C forms are kept. The Quality Standards wall: a large display showing current case satisfaction rates, updated in real time, showing the Bureau's monthly average hovering at 71%.
2. **Sound:** The specific murmur of people explaining other people's lives in narrative terms: "His story has a classic third-act problem, he peaked emotionally in Act 2 and hasn't found a new direction," spoken in the same tone as a weather report. The notification sound when a case reaches a critical narrative junction: a small chime that every agent recognizes and that causes everyone within earshot to look at their neighbor's screen.
3. **Touch and Smell:** The case files: standard manila folders for closed cases, blue for active, and red for remediation, stacked in configurations that experienced agents can read like a landscape. The Bureau's specific institutional smell: old paper, mediocre coffee, and the faint sweetness of the reward candy the Division Chief keeps in a dish that is always somehow full despite never being restocked.

## 2. The Bureau

**The Regional Office** handles approximately three hundred active cases at any given time. Division Chief Maren is the administrative head: competent, slightly overwhelmed, and deeply committed to the Bureau's mission in the specific way of someone who has been doing this long enough to know that it matters even when it is frustrating.

**The Quality Standards Division** reviews closed cases and determines whether the ending meets Bureau standards. Their criteria are a classified document. The agents who have read parts of it describe the criteria as "narrative common sense" except when they don't, which is often. Senior QSD reviewer Dax has the highest rejection rate in the Bureau's history and considers this a point of professional pride.

**The Case Assignment Desk** matches agents to cases based on an algorithm that the Bureau's computational division designed in 1987 and that nobody has been able to update because the original programmer retired without documenting anything. The algorithm works approximately 70% of the time. The other 30% of the time, cases are reassigned manually by the desk clerk, Bren, who has been doing this for twenty-two years and who claims to have a better success rate than the algorithm.

**The Remediation Department** handles cases that were closed with scores below 3 and must be reopened. The Department has its own culture: a resigned, darkly comic professionalism born from spending every day working on stories that already went wrong once. Their newsletter is the best-written internal publication in the Bureau.

## 3. Factions

**The Narrative Interventionists** are the mainstream Bureau agents who believe in working within the client's existing story: facilitating conversations, engineering coincidences, and nudging circumstances toward earned conclusions. They are philosophically opposed to large interventions because they believe unearned endings create quality issues.

**The Realists** are the agents who believe the Bureau's Quality Standards are too idealistic: sometimes a satisfying ending for the client is not a satisfying ending by narrative standards, and the Bureau should prioritize the client's actual experience over the QSD's aesthetic preferences. Their informal leader, a senior agent named Sela, has been filing policy objections for eight years.

**The Quality Standards Division** considers itself above faction politics. The QSD's actual position is that narrative quality and client welfare are the same thing in the long run, and that an ending that feels contrived will feel wrong to the client even if they cannot articulate why. Dax believes this deeply. The rest of the Bureau believes Dax is genuinely impossible to work with.

**The Clients** are the people whose cases are active. Most of them do not know the Bureau exists. Bureau agents operate without disclosure, which creates its own category of ethical complications that the Bureau has a specific committee for.

## 4. Unique Mechanic: Satisfaction Score

Track each case's **Satisfaction Score** from 0 to 6.

**Raising Satisfaction (+1 each):**
- Client makes a meaningful decision that advances their narrative toward closure
- A long-avoided conversation is successfully facilitated
- A narrative obstacle is resolved in a way that feels earned rather than convenient
- The client demonstrates genuine growth that the story has prepared them for

**Lowering Satisfaction (-1 each):**
- A coincidence is engineered that is too convenient to be plausible
- The client makes a decision that creates new complications rather than resolving existing ones
- An agent intervenes directly rather than facilitating the client's own agency
- The QSD identifies a structural narrative problem in the case's current direction

**Case closure:** A case closes when the client's narrative reaches a natural stopping point. If the Score is 5-6, the case is filed as Exemplary. 3-4 is Satisfactory. Below 3 triggers automatic QSD review.

## 5. Character Archetypes

### The New Agent

You completed Bureau training four months ago and you have been assigned your first solo case: a retired lighthouse keeper whose story the Case Assignment Algorithm describes as "unresolved second-act conflict with estranged daughter, probable tragic ending without intervention." You have read the file twelve times. You have attended three sensitivity training sessions. You have also, technically, already met the client's daughter at a coffee shop, which your supervisor is going to describe as either a promising narrative coincidence or a serious conflict of interest depending on how the next development goes.

- **Drive:** *"Close your first case with a Satisfactory rating without creating the kind of coincidence that gets your ending returned by the QSD."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 2 | Mind 2 | Presence 3

### The QSD Reviewer

You are Dax. You have rejected forty-seven cases this year. You know what a good ending looks like, and you know that most of what the Bureau produces falls short of it, not because the agents are lazy but because they are working under case quotas that incentivize speed over quality. You have been trying to change the quota system for six years. You are also aware that your reputation within the Bureau is approximately equal parts respected and despised, and that this situation is itself a narrative setup that you are not sure how to resolve.

- **Drive:** *"Get the quota system changed before it produces enough bad case closures to damage the Bureau's credibility with the Ministry, without destroying your working relationships in the process."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 1 | Mind 4 | Presence 2

### The Remediation Specialist

You have been working in Remediation for nine years. You are very good at it. You are also the only person in the Bureau who has read all forty-seven of Dax's rejection memos and agreed with every one of them, which has created a professional solidarity between you and the QSD that is approximately as useful as it sounds. Your current caseload includes a case that was originally filed in 1987 and has been in remediation through four different agents, which means you inherited not just a broken story but three previous agents' attempts to fix it, which broke it in new ways.

- **Drive:** *"Find the original narrative thread in the 1987 case, before the case's age causes it to be administratively archived as unresolvable."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 3 | Mind 2 | Presence 2

## 6. Starter Scenario and Why It Matters

**The Algorithm Conflict:** The Case Assignment Algorithm has matched three active cases that appear, on review, to be parts of the same story: three people whose narrative threads are clearly interlinked but who have been assigned to three different agents who are not coordinating. If the cases close independently, all three will probably score below 3. If they are merged into a single case, someone needs to determine which agent leads it, how the merged case is classified for quota purposes, and how to explain to the QSD why three active cases are being combined without their prior approval. The characters must untangle the assignment problem while keeping all three clients' narratives progressing, without triggering a remediation review before the cases even close.

**Why It Matters:** The Bureau of Happy Endings asks whether narrative satisfaction is something that can be administered. The Bureau believes it can, and it is probably right often enough to justify its existence. The comedy is in the gap between the complexity of human stories and the procedural frameworks the Bureau applies to them. The setting is about the specific absurdity of bureaucratic institutions that genuinely matter.
