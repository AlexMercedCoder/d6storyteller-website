> *"I have lived on Maple Drive for eleven years. The house at number 14 has been there the entire time. I am certain of this. I am also looking at a photograph from 2019 that shows an empty lot where number 14 stands. I do not know how to reconcile these two facts. I am also noticing that my kitchen has a door I did not install."*
> - Statement of Ellen Marsh, Maple Drive resident

## 1. Setting Overview & Tone

The house is ordinary. That is the worst part. It does not creak, glow, bleed, or produce sounds in the night. It sits on its lot like every other house on the street: maintained, unremarkable, and occupied by the conviction of every neighbor that it has always been there. The locked room on the second floor is the only anomaly visible from outside: a window with curtains that are always drawn, in a room that no floor plan accounts for.

The characters are investigators, journalists, neighbors, or specialists who have tracked the house across multiple locations. Each appearance follows the same pattern: arrival, integration, escalation, the room opens, and then the house is gone, replaced by an empty lot and a neighborhood of people who are trying to remember whether they always had three bedrooms or whether the third one appeared last Tuesday.

**Tone:** Quiet, domestic, and deeply unsettling. The horror is not spectacular. It is the slow dissolution of certainty: the growing suspicion that your home, your street, your memory of last Thursday's dinner, might not be entirely yours. The house does not attack. It influences. The damage it leaves behind is not physical but architectural: houses that have been near it develop rooms, doors, and hallways that were not part of the original construction.

**Sensory Pillars:**
1. **Sight:** A perfectly normal house on a perfectly normal street, distinguished only by the feeling that it is slightly too present, too vivid against its surroundings, as if it is rendered at a higher resolution than the buildings around it. The locked room's window: always curtained, always dark, except for a single moment each evening when a light appears and moves across the curtain as if someone inside is carrying a lamp.
2. **Sound:** The absence of sound from the house. No plumbing noise, no settling, no wind through gaps. The house is acoustically perfect, which is acoustically wrong: real houses make noise. The only sound associated with the house is a faint click, heard by neighbors at irregular intervals, which sounds like a lock turning. Nobody can identify which lock.
3. **Touch & Smell:** The red front door is warm regardless of weather. The key to the locked room, which does not exist but which neighbors occasionally find in their pockets, kitchen drawers, or jacket linings, is cold. The smell inside the house is clean and neutral, like a display home, which is unsettling because occupied houses smell like the people who live in them. This one smells like nothing, which is a smell of its own.

## 2. The Neighborhood

**The House Itself** is a two-story colonial: white clapboard, green shutters, red door, small front porch. Its interior is furnished in a style that matches the neighborhood's general aesthetic. The first floor is accessible and unremarkable. The stairs to the second floor are normal. The hallway at the top of the stairs has three doors: two open (a bedroom and a bathroom, both generic) and one locked. The locked door has no keyhole on the outside.

**The Adjacent Properties** are the houses closest to number 14. Their residents are the first to experience Room Pressure effects: a new closet that was not in the lease, a hallway that seems longer than it was last month, a door in the basement that opens onto a room the homeowner does not remember building. The changes are small, domestic, and increasingly difficult to dismiss.

**The Empty Lot** is what the house leaves behind when it moves. The lot is vacant, level, and indistinguishable from any undeveloped property. Neighbors who lived next to the house for months cannot remember what stood on the lot. Photographs taken before the house arrived show the empty space. Photographs taken during the house's occupancy show the house as if it had always been there.

**The Previous Sites** are the locations where the house has appeared before. An investigator who tracks the pattern can visit former neighborhoods and interview residents who experienced the house's effects. The symptoms are consistent: altered architecture, misplaced memories, and a lingering sense that something in the neighborhood's geometry is slightly wrong, even years after the house has gone.

## 3. Factions

**The Pattern Investigators** are a loose network of researchers, journalists, and former neighbors who have tracked the house across multiple appearances. Their coordinator, a journalist named Sasha Reeves, maintains a database of sightings, symptoms, and outcomes. Reeves has documented fourteen confirmed appearances over twenty years. She does not know what the house is. She knows what it does, and she is trying to find it before the locked room opens again.

**The Current Neighbors** are the residents of the street where the house currently sits. They remember the house as always having been there, which makes conversations with the investigators frustrating for both parties. The most receptive neighbor, a retired architect named Tom Falk, has noticed that his own house's floor plan no longer matches the blueprints he filed with the county, and he is willing to consider explanations that his training does not support.

**The Municipal Records Office** maintains property records that include the house at number 14, which means the house has altered the city's official documents. Records clerk Dani Wen discovered the discrepancy during a routine audit: the lot at number 14 was registered as vacant in January and as an occupied residential property in March, with no construction permits, no sale, and no deed transfer. Wen is investigating quietly because she does not want to file a report that says a house forged its own paperwork.

**The Room** is not a faction in the traditional sense, but it has agency. The locked room on the second floor responds to attention: investigating the house raises Room Pressure, which gives the room more influence over the surrounding area. The room wants to be opened. The pattern suggests that every previous time the room has been opened, the person who opened it was changed, the house moved, and the neighborhood's architecture was permanently altered. What the room wants with the people who open it is the investigation's central question.

## 4. Unique Mechanic: Room Pressure

Track **Room Pressure** from 0 to 6, starting at 1 (the house has recently arrived).

**Pressure rises (+1 each):**
- Time passes (Pressure rises by 1 every two sessions naturally)
- Someone enters the house and investigates
- A neighbor notices and discusses the architectural changes in their own home
- The locked room receives direct attention (listening at the door, examining the window, attempting to open it)

**Pressure effects:**
| Pressure | Neighborhood Effect |
|----------|-------------------|
| 1 | Subtle. Neighbors occasionally misremember small details. A drawer that opens the wrong way. |
| 2 | Noticeable. Adjacent houses develop minor architectural additions: a closet, a cabinet, a shelf that was not there last week. |
| 3 | Disruptive. Neighbors experience lost time (minutes, not hours). Doors in adjacent houses begin sticking or opening by themselves. |
| 4 | Structural. Adjacent houses gain rooms. Hallways lengthen. Windows show views that do not match the exterior. |
| 5 | Critical. The neighborhood's geography shifts. Streets curve where they were straight. The house is visible from angles that should be blocked by other buildings. |
| 6 | The locked room opens on its own. Whatever is inside is revealed. The house moves to a new location within days. |

**Reducing Pressure (-1 each):**
- Removing the house's connection to the neighborhood (correcting forged records, helping neighbors recognize the false memories)
- Understanding the room's pattern well enough to interrupt it
- Convincing the neighborhood to collectively disengage from the house

## 5. Character Archetypes

### The Pattern Journalist

You have been tracking the house for four years across three cities. You have a database, a map of previous appearances, and a growing collection of interviews with former neighbors. You are the closest thing this investigation has to an expert. You are also aware that your obsession with the house has begun affecting your own apartment: a hallway that you are fairly sure was not there when you signed the lease.

- **Drive:** *"Document the pattern completely enough to predict or prevent the next opening, and figure out whether the house is following me or I am following it."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 2 | Mind 3 | Presence 2

### The Affected Neighbor

You live two doors down from number 14. Last month, your house gained a room. It is a small study, fully furnished, located between your bedroom and the bathroom. Your family uses it. Your children do homework in it. It does not appear on your floor plan, your mortgage documents, or any photograph of your house taken before March. You are starting to question the room, and you are discovering that questioning the room makes it angry.

- **Drive:** *"Understand what is happening to my home before my family stops noticing that the changes are wrong."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 2 | Finesse 1 | Mind 2 | Presence 3

### The Records Investigator

You work in the city's records office and you found the discrepancy: a house that appeared in municipal records without any of the documentation that a real property requires. You are methodical, detail-oriented, and increasingly disturbed. The records are not just wrong. They have been altered retroactively, as if the house reached into the filing system and wrote itself into history. You are auditing every property on the street, and the audit is revealing that several other houses have irregularities that nobody noticed until you looked.

- **Drive:** *"Trace the forgery back to its source and restore the records before the house's alterations become permanent."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 2 | Mind 3 | Presence 2

## 6. Starter Scenario & Why It Matters

**The Key in the Drawer:** Tom Falk, the retired architect, has found a key in his kitchen drawer. It is cold, iron, and shaped for a lock that does not match any door in his house. He is certain it is the key to the locked room at number 14. His certainty is not based on evidence. It is based on a knowledge he cannot explain, the same way he knows that his house had two bedrooms last year and has three now. Sasha Reeves has arrived in the neighborhood and is asking questions that the neighbors find confusing. Dani Wen has discovered that three other houses on the street have property records that do not match their physical footprints. The Room Pressure is at 3 and rising. The characters must decide whether to use the key, whether the key is genuine or a trap, and what to do about a neighborhood that is slowly being rewritten by a house that has been doing this for decades and has never been stopped.

**Why It Matters:** The House on Every Street asks what happens when something impossible integrates itself into ordinary life so thoroughly that questioning it feels like the unreasonable act. The house does not invade. It belongs. The neighbors remember it. The records confirm it. The only evidence that something is wrong is the locked room, the architectural changes in adjacent buildings, and the specific discomfort of knowing that your home has a room you did not build and cannot explain. The setting is about the horror of normalization: the way extraordinary things become ordinary when they are patient enough, and the difficulty of questioning something that everyone around you insists has always been there.
