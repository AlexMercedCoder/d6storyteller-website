> *"Three parties. The first holds the current legal title, purchased at auction in 1987 from a collection that acquired it during the occupation. The second is the artist's estate, which contests the occupation-era transfer on grounds of duress. The third is a private family that claims the artist gave the piece to their grandmother personally in 1938, which predates all of the other claims. We have documentation supporting all three positions. We have a client who is none of them, who simply wants to know where the piece is. I find this more interesting than usual."*
> - Sable, Museum of Borrowed Things, Case File Note

## 1. Setting Overview and Tone

The team operates with a specific professional identity: they are, for public purposes, researchers and consultants who assist in the documentation of art provenance. This identity is legitimate: they do this work, and they do it well. The work also creates access to exactly the information needed for the other work, which is less documentable.

The team has three members and a specific operational culture: no violence, no operations where the ethical claim is not at least arguable, and a requirement that at least two of the three members agree on an operation's legitimacy before it proceeds.

**Tone:** Thoughtful heist drama with genuine ethical weight. Every case involves research, negotiation, and the specific intelligence of people who know exactly how complicated "who does this belong to" can be.

**Sensory Pillars:**
1. **Sight:** The team's workspace: a rented office that functions as the Museum's administrative headquarters, with full provenance research equipment, an art history library, and a physical archive of their previous cases. The pieces they work with: objects with histories layered deeper than their surfaces suggest. The private gallery the current piece is housed in: well-secured, climate-controlled, and belonging to a party who is aware that their claim to it is potentially contested.
2. **Sound:** The research process: methodical, iterative, and involving a specific vocabulary borrowed from art history, legal practice, and the private intelligence world. The team's internal case discussions: direct, occasionally heated, and held to a standard where disagreement is productive rather than destructive.
3. **Touch and Smell:** The physical research materials: old documents, photographic records, auction house catalogs from seventy years ago, and the occasional diplomatic correspondence that has escaped official archives. The pieces themselves: Sable can assess an object's age, condition, and certain aspects of its history through careful examination in ways that document-only research cannot replicate.

## 2. The Team

**Sable** is the team lead: provenance researcher, assessment specialist, and the person who first evaluates whether a case meets the team's operational standard. Her background is academic (art history doctorate, specialization in 20th century European displacement), and her transition from legitimate research to the current operation was gradual and practical. She still publishes academic articles under her research identity.

**Nori** is the access specialist: the team member who handles the physical operations. Nori's background is in private security, and the transition to the other side of that work was prompted by a specific case where the object being protected was being protected for the wrong party. Nori does not discuss this case.

**Bren** is the documentation specialist: the person who creates and validates the paperwork that supports the team's operations and, when necessary, their cover. Bren's background is in document authentication for a major auction house, which means they know exactly how provenance documentation is constructed and how to identify when it has been constructed to mislead.

## 3. Factions

**The Three Claimants:** The current legal titleholder is a Swiss collector who purchased in good faith in 1987 and has maintained the piece legally since. The artist's estate has contested occupation-era transfers for thirty years and has won four such cases. The private family has documentation that predates all other claims but has never pursued it legally for reasons that may be related to the family's complicated wartime history.

**The Unknown Client** is the party who retained the team to locate the piece without providing a clear purpose. The client has a specific interest in the piece that they have not disclosed, which is the source of the team's current operational caution: they have found the piece, they have documented all three claims, and they are now deciding whether to disclose their findings to their client before understanding why the client wants to know.

**The Art World Establishment** is the background context: auction houses, museum acquisition departments, insurance companies, and the professional provenance research community that the team's legitimate research identity gives them access to. This community has complex interests in how occupation-era claims are resolved and is not uniformly supportive of the artist's estate's approach.

**The International Claims Foundation** is a legitimate organization that provides legal support for art restitution claims. Their staff lawyer, Dax, has been tracking the piece that the team is currently investigating for a different client and is about to file a claim that will make the piece publicly contested, which will change the team's operational context significantly.

## 4. Unique Mechanic: Chain of Claim

Track each claimant's **Claim Strength** from 0 to 5.

| Strength | Claim Status |
|----------|-------------|
| 5 | Clear, documented, legally actionable. |
| 4 | Strong. Significant documentation, clear moral position, legal pathway. |
| 3 | Credible. Partial documentation, arguable position. |
| 2 | Contested. Evidence present but disputed. Multiple interpretations. |
| 1 | Weak. Primarily testimonial or circumstantial. |
| 0 | No viable claim. |

**Operations affecting Claim Strength:**
- Discovering primary documentation for a claim: +1 to that claim
- Identifying evidence that undermines a claim: -1 to that claim
- A legal filing that contests a claim: forces reassessment
- Recovery of the piece by one party: strengthens that party's practical position regardless of Claim Strength

**Resolution:** Operations that produce a clear strongest claim (one party at 4-5 and others at 2 or below) have a durable resolution path. Operations that produce balanced claims (two or more parties at 3-4) require negotiated resolution. Operations that produce no clear claim (all at 2 or below) require legal process to resolve.

## 5. Character Archetypes

### The Researcher

You are Sable. You have been documenting provenance cases for twelve years in the academic world and seven in the current operation. You find the current case genuinely interesting rather than simply complicated, which is the sign that it is probably more complicated than it currently appears. You have identified all three claims. You have reached a preliminary assessment that the private family's claim is the strongest on the merits, the artist's estate's claim is the most legally actionable, and the current titleholder's claim is the most legally solid. You have also noted that the unknown client has not asked for this assessment: they asked for the piece's location, which is a meaningfully different request.

- **Drive:** *"Complete the provenance assessment with enough clarity to make an ethical recommendation about the piece's resolution, and determine what the client's actual purpose is before disclosing the location."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 2 | Mind 4 | Presence 1

### The Access Specialist

You are Nori. You handle the physical operations: the access to private archives, the examination of pieces in secured locations, and the recoveries when the team determines that recovery is the right outcome. You have a specific physical skill set and a professional ethic that limits what that skill set is used for. The current case has not yet reached the physical operation stage: you are waiting for the research phase to produce a clear direction. You are also concerned that the International Claims Foundation's imminent filing will produce a legal context that changes what physical access is available, which means if there is anything to examine at the piece's current location, it needs to happen before the filing.

- **Drive:** *"Conduct a physical examination of the piece at its current location before the Claims Foundation filing changes the legal environment, and determine whether the examination produces evidence that changes any of the three Claim Strength assessments."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 4 | Mind 2 | Presence 1

### The Documentation Specialist

You are Bren. You have authenticated and, when necessary, constructed documentation for eleven years. You know what genuine provenance documentation looks like and you know what artificially aged, retroactively constructed provenance documentation looks like. You have been reviewing the three claimants' supporting documents and you have identified something that you have not yet shared with the rest of the team: the current titleholder's 1987 auction documentation appears to be genuine, but it references a provenance verification letter that you cannot locate in any archive. The absence of that letter is either an innocent archival gap or evidence that it was never issued, which would mean the 1987 auction's provenance was accepted without verification. This finding would affect both the current titleholder's Claim Strength and the auction house's current liability exposure.

- **Drive:** *"Locate the referenced provenance letter or confirm its absence, determine the legal implications for the current titleholder and the auction house, and decide whether this finding changes the team's operational recommendation."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 2 | Mind 4 | Presence 1

## 6. Starter Scenario and Why It Matters

**The Three-Way:** The team has located the piece, completed preliminary research, and identified all three claims. The International Claims Foundation has not yet filed, which gives them approximately two weeks before the piece becomes publicly contested. The unknown client has not been told the location yet. Bren's missing letter finding has produced a fourth consideration: if the 1987 auction's provenance was unverified, then the current titleholder is a good-faith purchaser from a flawed chain, which affects their moral position even if it doesn't change their legal standing. Nori has confirmed physical access to the piece is available now but may not be available once the Foundation files. The team must decide: tell the client the location, complete the physical examination first, wait for legal clarity, or pursue a negotiated resolution with one or more claimants directly. Every option has costs. None is clearly right.

**Why It Matters:** The Museum of Borrowed Things is about the specific ethical challenge of art restitution: the question of who an object belongs to when multiple parties have partial legitimate claims, and whether "who has the legal title" is the same question as "who should have it." The team does not answer this question for the world. They answer it, case by case, for themselves, and they hold themselves to their own standard whether or not the law agrees with it.
