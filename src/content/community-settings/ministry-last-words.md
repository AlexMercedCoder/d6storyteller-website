## 1. Setting Overview & Tone

The Ministry occupies the fourth floor of a government building between the Department of Vital Records and the Office of Unclaimed Property. Its staff of twelve clerks works in shifts, because people die at all hours. The job is straightforward: attend the dying, record their final words, file the transcript, and archive the recording. The job becomes complicated when the words describe something that has not happened yet.

Approximately 8% of recorded last words contain predictive content. The Ministry has verified this statistic over forty years of operation. The predictions are specific enough to be useful and vague enough to be legally meaningless: a dying plumber describes a robbery that occurs three weeks later at a location he has never visited; a retired teacher recites a conversation between two people she has never met, a conversation that takes place five days after her funeral.

**Tone:** Bureaucratic, morally exhausting, and haunted by ordinary obligation. The horror is not the dying or the predictions. The horror is the gap between knowledge and authority: the clerks know what is coming and cannot do anything about it because their filing system does not include a form for "crime that has not happened yet."

**Sensory Pillars:**
1. **Sight:** The Ministry's office: fluorescent lighting, metal filing cabinets, desks arranged in rows, a wall clock that the clerks have started watching with uncomfortable attention. The dying: hospital beds, living rooms, emergency rooms, and occasionally the street, wherever the clerks are dispatched to record. The files: manila folders with typed transcripts, each one stamped with a classification (Personal, Confession, Predictive A through D).
2. **Sound:** The voice of a dying person delivering their final words, which is intimate in a way that no other speech is: unguarded, unpracticed, and occasionally aimed at an audience the clerk cannot see. The scratch of a pen recording in real time. The click of a cassette recorder. The particular silence in the office after a clerk returns from a predictive recording, when everyone knows the words are in the file and nobody wants to be the first to read them.
3. **Touch & Smell:** The institutional smell of the Ministry: floor cleaner, old coffee, and the specific mustiness of files that have been stored in cabinets for decades. The weight of a predictive file, which feels the same as any other file but which the clerks carry differently, as if the contents exert a gravity that ordinary paperwork does not. The cold of a hospital room at 3 AM, where a clerk sits beside a stranger and waits for the words to come.

## 2. The Ministry

**The Recording Room** is where clerks transcribe and classify new entries. It is a quiet room with individual carrels, each equipped with a cassette player and a typewriter. Clerks listen to their recordings, transcribe the words, and assign a classification. The room is where the emotional weight of the job settles: a clerk who has just recorded a dying grandmother's farewell sits next to a clerk who is transcribing a prediction of a murder that will occur next Tuesday.

**The Predictive Archive** is a locked section of the filing room that contains every prediction the Ministry has recorded. The archive is organized chronologically, not by content, which means finding a relevant prediction requires searching through decades of filed words. The archivist, a woman named Carol Sato, has been creating a cross-reference index on her own time. Her index is extensive, unofficial, and increasingly alarming.

**The Dispatch Office** coordinates the clerks' assignments: which hospitals, hospices, and emergency calls to respond to. Dispatch is managed by Supervisor Henk, a career civil servant who treats the job with the same procedural care he would give any government recording function. Henk does not read the predictive files. He considers this a professional boundary. His staff considers it willful avoidance.

**The Basement Storage** contains the oldest files, dating back to the Ministry's founding. The basement is cold, poorly lit, and organized by a filing system that predates the current staff. A clerk who ventures into the basement to retrieve an old file will discover that the earliest predictions are not filed under Predictive at all. They are filed under a category that no longer exists in the current system: "Instruction."

## 3. Factions

**The Working Clerks** are the Ministry's twelve staff members. They record, transcribe, file, and try not to think too hard about the 8% that describes the future. Senior clerk Maren Dahl has been at the Ministry for fifteen years and has recorded over 3,000 last words. She is methodical, compassionate, and privately maintaining a list of predictions that came true. The list is long. She has not shown it to anyone because she does not know what showing it would accomplish.

**The Ethics Office** is a single lawyer named James Pratt, assigned to the Ministry part-time to advise on legal questions related to the recordings. Pratt's position is that predictions cannot be acted upon because they are not evidence, do not meet any legal standard for probable cause, and would create a precedent that allows government action based on the words of dying strangers. Pratt is legally correct. He is also aware that three predictions he classified as non-actionable have since been verified by actual crimes.

**The Quiet Network** is an informal group of current and former clerks who have begun acting on predictions outside official channels. They do not call themselves anything. They share information, visit locations mentioned in predictions, and attempt to prevent crimes through indirect intervention: anonymous tips, strategic presence, and the careful deployment of information they are not supposed to use. The network's organizer is a retired clerk named Paz Mendez, who left the Ministry after a prediction she filed correctly described the death of her neighbor's child. She filed it three weeks before it happened. She did nothing. She has been doing something ever since.

**The Source** is not understood. The Ministry does not know why some dying people produce accurate predictions. Research has found no pattern: the predictive speakers are not psychic, not religiously affiliated, not geographically clustered, and not aware that their words contain information about the future. The predictions appear to come from somewhere outside the dying person, channeled through the act of dying itself. The Ministry's founder believed the predictions were a natural phenomenon. The Basement Storage files suggest the founder believed something more specific than that.

## 4. Unique Mechanic: Prophecy Accuracy

Track a shared **Prophecy Accuracy** score from 0 to 6, starting at 3.

**Raising Accuracy (+1 each):**
- A prediction is verified against an actual event
- Cross-referencing multiple predictions reveals a consistent pattern
- Acting on a prediction successfully prevents or mitigates the predicted event
- Recovering an old file from Basement Storage that provides historical verification

**Lowering Accuracy (-1 each):**
- A prediction fails to correspond to any verifiable event
- Acting on a prediction and being wrong (arriving at the predicted location and finding nothing)
- A prediction is deliberately falsified or altered in the files
- The Ethics Office successfully argues that a prediction should be reclassified as non-predictive

**Thresholds:**
| Accuracy | Ministry Status |
|----------|----------------|
| 5-6 | High confidence. Predictions are reliable enough to justify preventive action, if anyone with authority would authorize it. |
| 3-4 | Working baseline. Predictions are sometimes right, sometimes wrong. The Ministry functions but cannot convince outsiders of its value. |
| 1-2 | Low confidence. Funding is at risk. Staff morale drops. The Quiet Network becomes more active because official channels are failing. |
| 0 | The Ministry is defunded or discredited. The predictions continue. Nobody is recording them. |

## 5. Character Archetypes

### The Senior Clerk

You have been recording last words for fifteen years. You are gentle with the dying, precise with your transcriptions, and haunted by the predictions you filed correctly that nobody acted on. You maintain a private list of verified predictions. The list is evidence of something. You are not sure what it is evidence for, but you know it should not stay in your desk drawer forever.

- **Drive:** *"Decide what to do with fifteen years of proof that the dying can see the future, knowing that the system I work in has no process for what I know."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 2 | Mind 3 | Presence 2

### The Quiet Interventionist

You left the Ministry three years ago after filing a prediction you could have acted on and did not. Now you act on them. You operate outside official channels, using information from current clerks who share files they are not supposed to share. You have prevented two crimes. You have also arrived at three locations and found nothing. You do not know whether the empty arrivals mean the predictions were wrong or whether your presence changed the outcome.

- **Drive:** *"Prevent the next one, even if the system says I have no right to try."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 2 | Finesse 2 | Mind 1 | Presence 3

### The Ethics Lawyer

You advise the Ministry on legal compliance. Your job is to ensure that the recordings are handled properly and that nobody acts on information that does not meet legal standards. You are good at this job. You are also sitting on a file that correctly predicted the last three verified crimes in your district, and your legal opinion that the predictions are non-actionable is becoming harder to defend to yourself.

- **Drive:** *"Find a legal framework that lets the Ministry act on what it knows without creating a system where the government imprisons people for crimes that dying strangers say they will commit."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 2 | Mind 3 | Presence 2

## 6. Starter Scenario & Why It Matters

**File 4,271:** Hilda Crace's prediction about "the man in the gray coat" and "the Halford girl" has nine days before the predicted date. The Predictive Archive contains no prior mention of a Halford family. Carol Sato's cross-reference index, however, has identified three previous predictions over the last decade that mention a "gray coat" in connection with missing children. The predictions were filed by three different clerks who recorded three different dying people in three different hospitals. None of them knew each other. The Ethics Office says the Ministry cannot contact law enforcement based on a dying woman's statement. The Quiet Network wants to find the house on Vine before the twelfth. Maren Dahl has opened the Basement Storage and found that the earliest files, the ones classified as "Instruction," contain a prediction from 1962 that describes the gray coat, the park, and a pattern that has been repeating for longer than the Ministry has existed. The characters must decide how to act on information they are not authorized to use, in a system that treats prediction as a filing category rather than a warning.

**Why It Matters:** The Ministry of Last Words asks what obligation comes with knowledge you cannot prove. The clerks know what is coming. The system they work in does not have a form for prevention. The setting is about the moral weight of information, the limitations of institutions designed for ordinary problems, and the specific frustration of people who work inside a system that functions exactly as designed and is not designed to save anyone.
