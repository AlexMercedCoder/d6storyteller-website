## 1. Setting Overview and Tone

Marel began as a waystation: a natural harbor on an unclaimed island where ships could take on water and make minor repairs without port fees or inspections. The first permanent residents were the workers who maintained that service. Their children built the first permanent structures. Their grandchildren built the Harbor Council and the Marel Charter: a set of governance principles that have no legal standing in any recognized court and have governed the island effectively for sixty years.

Marel is not an anarchist fantasy or a pirate haven. It is a functioning community with courts, commerce, property law, and a genuine civic identity built by people who had no other options and made something worth having.

**Tone:** Politically serious and community-focused. The setting is about self-governance, the legitimacy of institutions built by people rather than granted by states, and the specific vulnerability of communities whose existence is never on the map.

**Sensory Pillars:**
1. **Sight:** The harbor itself: a natural amphitheater of rock with deep water right up to the dock walls, the city climbing the surrounding hillsides in a patchwork of architecture from every culture that has contributed permanent residents. The flag situation: Marel flies no national flag. Its harbor entrance is marked with a simple geometric pattern that its residents designed and the harbor communities of three other unclaimed ports have adopted in solidarity.
2. **Sound:** The harbor's multilingual commerce: Marel has no official language and most residents are functional in at least three. The Council sessions: conducted in a rotation of languages with summary translations, which makes them longer than they would otherwise be and more representative than most formal government meetings. The sound of the harbor at work: no different from any other working port, which is the point.
3. **Touch and Smell:** The harbor's salt air mixed with the smells of cooking from forty different culinary traditions on the hillside streets. The harbor district's specific texture: stone dock walls built to last, the work of people who planned to stay.

## 2. The Harbor

**The Deep Anchorage** is Marel's geographic advantage: a natural basin with forty feet of water at low tide, capable of handling the largest vessels in the known world. The anchorage is why the four nations have started paying attention. A deep-water harbor in this location would give any fleet significant strategic range extension.

**The Harbor Council Hall** is the community's governing center: a large meeting space built from materials donated by six different cultural communities in the harbor's third generation. The Council meets three times weekly for regular business and any time a member calls an emergency session, which is frequent.

**The Marel Market** is the economic engine: a permanent market operating seven days a week where goods of unclear provenance find new owners without documentation requirements. The Market is the reason ships come to Marel when they cannot go elsewhere. Its keeper, a woman named Bren, has been managing it for twenty years and has an informal intelligence network that gives her better information about the regional maritime situation than most formal intelligence services.

**The Unbuilt Quarter** is the northeastern section of the island where no permanent structures exist by mutual agreement: land reserved for new arrivals who need somewhere to stay while they determine whether Marel is their permanent home. The Quarter has informal services: a communal kitchen, a clinic, and a rotating crew of long-term residents who help newcomers understand the Charter.

## 3. Factions

**The Harbor Council** is the community's elected governance body. Council Chair Sela Marsh is in her third term: thoughtful, experienced, and currently managing the most serious threat to Marel's existence in the community's history. The Council is divided between pragmatists who want to negotiate a limited recognition arrangement with one or more of the four nations, and principled resisters who believe any formal recognition will inevitably lead to sovereignty claims.

**The Four Claimant Nations** are each arguing that Marel falls within their historical or geographic sphere of influence. None of them have ever claimed it, taxed it, or administered it. All of them are now arguing, simultaneously, that the community of three thousand people who built the island's infrastructure over three generations are squatters on strategically significant territory. Their envoys have arrived in the harbor and are presenting their cases to the Harbor Council, which has not invited them and does not have an established procedure for uninvited diplomatic missions.

**The Commercial Community** is the merchant network that has depended on Marel's no-questions harbor for a generation. Their interests are complicated: they want Marel to remain operational and non-restrictive, which means they want the sovereignty claims defeated, but they are also not willing to provide material support that would draw their own nations' attention to their Marel business.

**The Charter Advocates** are the community members who see the current crisis as an opportunity rather than a threat: the moment to formalize Marel's governance, codify the Charter into a document that can be presented to international legal forums, and argue that a community of three thousand people with sixty years of self-governance has a legitimate claim to recognition on its own terms, not as the possession of any surrounding nation. Their leader, a legal scholar named Dax who came to Marel as a refugee and has stayed as a citizen, is drafting a sovereignty declaration.

## 4. Unique Mechanic: Recognition Politics

Track **Political Recognition** from 0 to 6 (Marel starts at 1).

**Raising Recognition (+1 each):**
- Negotiate a formal non-aggression or trade agreement with a recognized nation
- Successfully resist a claim through legal or diplomatic means
- Establish a formal relationship with another unrecognized community
- Have the Charter recognized by an international legal body

**Lowering Recognition (-1 each):**
- A nation successfully argues Marel is within its jurisdiction
- A high-profile criminal operation uses Marel's no-questions policy and creates diplomatic pressure
- The Harbor Council accepts conditions from a recognized nation that limit its operational independence
- Military action or credible military threat forces a capitulation

**Recognition thresholds:**
| Recognition | Status |
|-------------|--------|
| 5-6 | Recognized community. Marel has diplomatic status. Claims are legally contested. |
| 3-4 | Acknowledged community. Nations deal with Marel pragmatically but without formal recognition. |
| 1-2 | Operational but precarious. Marel exists by tolerance rather than right. |
| 0 | Claimed. A nation has established effective sovereignty. The Charter community continues under occupation. |

## 5. Character Archetypes

### The Harbor Council Chair

You have built a political career in a polity that is not supposed to exist. Your skills are real: you can manage a fractious council, negotiate with envoys from nations that want to erase your community, and maintain the civic culture that makes Marel worth defending. You are also terrified, in the specific way of a person who understands exactly how precarious the thing they love is. You need to navigate four simultaneous sovereignty claims without surrendering the independence that makes Marel what it is.

- **Drive:** *"Keep Marel ungoverned by anyone but its own people long enough to establish the legal and political precedents that make its existence durable rather than merely tolerated."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 2 | Mind 2 | Presence 3

### The Charter Scholar

You are Dax. You came to Marel as a refugee from a nation whose legal system classified your existence as a crime. You have spent fifteen years studying governance, international law, and the specific question of whether self-organized communities have rights that precede and supersede national claims. You are drafting a sovereignty declaration that argues they do. You need the Harbor Council to formally adopt it, which requires convincing Sela that a formal claim is less risky than continued ambiguity.

- **Drive:** *"Complete the sovereignty declaration and convince the Council to adopt it before one of the four claimant nations moves from diplomatic pressure to military action."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 1 | Mind 4 | Presence 2

### The Market Keeper

You are Bren. You have been running the Marel Market for twenty years and you know more about the regional maritime situation than any of the envoys currently in the harbor. Your information network is not something you planned. It is something that accumulated because you talk to everyone and remember everything and have been doing it long enough that people tell you things because they know you will not misuse them. You are a market keeper who is about to become an intelligence asset whether you want to be or not.

- **Drive:** *"Use what you know to protect the community that gave you a place when you needed one, without transforming the market's trust-based information network into a political weapon that destroys what made it valuable."*
- **Approach Distribution:** Force 1 | Finesse 3 | Mind 2 | Presence 2

## 6. Starter Scenario and Why It Matters

**The Fleet in the Roads:** One of the four claimant nations has moved a naval squadron into the outer anchorage, within sight of the harbor but outside the harbor entrance. The squadron's flag captain has sent a polite communication explaining that the fleet is conducting "routine exercises in international waters" and has no territorial intent at this time. The Harbor Council is meeting in emergency session. The envoys from the other three nations are each arguing that the naval presence demonstrates that their rivals cannot be trusted with Marel's protection and that only their nation's governance can guarantee the community's security. Dax's sovereignty declaration is two weeks from completion. Bren's network has information about the fleet captain's actual orders that contradicts his communication. The characters must navigate the immediate crisis while advancing Marel's long-term legal position.

**Why It Matters:** Harbor of the Unclaimed is about the legitimacy of self-organized communities in a world built around state authority. Marel exists. Its people built it. They govern it. The nations surrounding it have never claimed it, never maintained it, never contributed to its infrastructure, and are now arguing that their geographic proximity gives them rights over what the community built. The setting is about the politics of recognition and the specific injustice of a world where existence requires a nation's permission.
